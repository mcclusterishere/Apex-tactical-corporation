# Internal Accounts and Money Transmission

*For the Treasurer, the Registrar General, and anyone proposing to build a payment
feature into this system. Read it before opening a member account, before agreeing
to hold money for anyone, and before writing a line of code that moves a balance
from one person to another.
Read `08-TREASURY-AND-FINANCIAL-CONTROL` first.*

## 1. The distinction, in one sentence

Handling the Kingdom's own money is book-keeping; handling other people's money
on its way to someone else is a licensed financial business, and doing it without
the licence is a federal felony. Everything below elaborates that sentence. The
line is not about amounts, technology, good faith, or whether anyone is charged a
fee. It is about whose money it is and where it is going.

## 2. What money transmission is

Federal law defines a money transmitting business at 31 U.S.C. § 5330 and defines
money services businesses at 31 C.F.R. § 1010.100(ff). The operative definition
for present purposes is the money transmitter at § 1010.100(ff)(5): a person who
provides money transmission services, meaning the acceptance of currency, funds,
or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission
of that currency, funds, or value to another location or person by any means.

Three features of that definition catch people out.

**"Or other value that substitutes for currency."** The rule is not limited to
dollars. Tokens, credits, points redeemable for goods, and convertible virtual
currency are inside it. FinCEN has said so directly in its guidance on virtual
currencies, FIN-2013-G001 and FIN-2019-G001.

**"By any means."** There is no requirement that money physically move, that a
wire be sent, or that anything leave the building. Adjusting two ledger balances
so that value has passed from one person to another is transmission.

**There is no activity threshold.** The de minimis thresholds in the money
services business definitions attach to dealers in foreign exchange, check
cashers, and issuers and sellers of money orders and traveller's cheques. Money
transmitters have none. A single transfer, once, for twenty dollars, as a favour,
can constitute the business.

The narrow exclusions at § 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii) — for certain payment processors
operating through a clearance and settlement system under agreement with the
seller, for persons providing only delivery or network access, and a short list of
others — are drafted tightly and are not a general escape hatch. Assume none
applies until counsel says which one does and why.

## 3. Registration, licensure, and the criminal provision

A money services business must register with FinCEN under 31 C.F.R. § 1022.380
within 180 days after the date on which it is established, renew that
registration every two years, and maintain a current list of its agents.
Registration is a filing rather than an approval, and it is free. Filing late,
after someone has asked, does not undo the period of non-registration.

Connecticut licenses money transmission separately, under Conn. Gen. Stat.
§ 36a-595 et seq., administered by the Banking Commissioner. A licence means an
application, a surety bond, minimum net worth, background checks on control
persons, permissible-investment requirements, examination, and annual reporting —
a real business undertaking with a compliance budget measured in tens of thousands
of dollars a year before anyone is paid to run it. Every other state whose
resident sends or receives applies its own law.

The criminal provision is 18 U.S.C. § 1960. It makes it an offence to conduct an
unlicensed money transmitting business, punishable by up to five years'
imprisonment, with forfeiture. Two of its features deserve emphasis. The statute
reaches a business that operates without a state licence where state law makes
unlicensed operation punishable — *whether or not the defendant knew that a
licence was required*. Ignorance of the licensing requirement is expressly
removed as a defence by the text of the statute. And it independently reaches
failure to comply with the FinCEN registration requirement under § 5330. Neither
branch requires that anyone be defrauded, that the money be dirty, or that the
operator profit.

## 4. What is not money transmission

None of the following is money transmission, and the Kingdom should do all of
them without hesitation.

Receiving tithes, offerings, donations, dues, and grant payments. That money
becomes the Kingdom's on receipt; there is no third party waiting for it.

Paying the Kingdom's own vendors, contractors, utilities, insurers, and staff,
and reimbursing a member against a receipt for something they bought for the
Kingdom. The Kingdom is spending its own funds for its own purposes.

Disbursing charitable assistance from the benevolence fund to a person in need,
including paying a recipient's bill directly, where the money is the Kingdom's
own charitable money given away on its own decision. What makes this safe is that
nobody handed the Kingdom money to be passed on; the Kingdom decided to give away
its own.

Maintaining a book record of what a member has contributed, what a member has
pledged, or what the Kingdom owes a member. A member account in this system is a
receivable and payable record — a statement of a relationship between the Kingdom
and one member — and nothing more. Issuing member statements from it is good
stewardship and is one of the controls in section 5 of the treasury document.

## 5. Where the line actually falls

The dangerous cases do not look dangerous. They look like kindness.

**Holding a member's money and paying their landlord.** This is money
transmission: funds accepted from one person and transmitted to another. The rent
being real, the member being grateful, and the Kingdom charging nothing change
nothing. Do it the safe way instead — either the member pays the landlord and the
Kingdom reimburses them from its own benevolence fund, or the Kingdom pays the
landlord directly out of its own funds as charitable assistance it decided to
give. Never take the member's money in first.

**Letting two members settle a debt through their Kingdom accounts.** Value moves
from one person to another across the Kingdom's rails. That is transmission, even
though the money never leaves the building and no cash is involved, because value
that substitutes for currency was accepted from one and made available to
another. This is the single feature most often requested and it is the one that
must not be built.

**A community currency that circulates.** If a Kingdom-issued credit can be spent
with third parties, it is value substituting for currency and its issuance and
redemption raise money transmission questions immediately. It also engages 18
U.S.C. § 336, which prohibits issuing notes for less than one dollar intended to
circulate as money, and 18 U.S.C. § 486 on coins passed as current money. A
closed voucher redeemable only by the Kingdom for the Kingdom's own goods and
services is a different animal — a gift certificate — but the moment it trades
between members it has stopped being one.

**Accepting money to send abroad to a member's family.** Textbook money
transmission, aggravated by cross-border recordkeeping and sanctions screening
obligations. A congregation collecting for relatives overseas is exactly what the
statute is aimed at. Refer the member to a licensed remitter.

**Passing through a designated gift.** A donor gives $5,000 for a named household
in difficulty. If the Kingdom is genuinely a conduit for a named individual, that
is transmission and, separately, the donor gets no charitable deduction, because
the gift is earmarked for a person rather than given to the Kingdom. If the
Kingdom accepts the money with full discretion over its use, subject only to a
stated charitable purpose, it is a restricted gift and the Kingdom spends it as
its own. Discretion is what separates the two, and it must be real and documented
at receipt.

## 6. Deposit-taking, which is a different regulator

An internal account that pays interest, or that a member may draw on demand,
stops looking like a receivable ledger and starts looking like a bank account.
Two regimes engage.

Deposit-taking is reserved to institutions chartered and supervised for the
purpose. Under 12 U.S.C. § 378(a)(2) it is unlawful for a person not subject to
examination and regulation under state or federal banking law to engage in the
business of receiving deposits subject to check or repayment on presentation of a
passbook, certificate of deposit, or other evidence of debt, or on the depositor's
request. Connecticut reserves the banking business to institutions organised and
supervised under Title 36a of the General Statutes.

And an interest-bearing demand obligation is presumptively a security. That is not
an analogy: *Reves v. Ernst & Young*, 494 U.S. 56 (1990), was itself about demand
notes issued by a cooperative to its own members at an attractive rate, and the
whole of `09-OBLIGATIONS-AND-SECURITIES` applies. An internal account paying a
return is therefore two regulated things at once. Pay no interest on any internal
account, promise no return, and describe no balance as redeemable on demand.

## 7. If the Kingdom ever did become a money services business

Registration is the beginning, not the end. The Bank Secrecy Act obligations that
follow are the actual cost: a written anti-money-laundering programme with a
designated compliance officer, training, and independent testing (31 U.S.C.
§ 5318(h); 31 C.F.R. § 1022.210); suspicious activity reports on transactions of
$2,000 or more that fit the reporting criteria, filed within thirty days and
never disclosed to the customer (31 C.F.R. § 1022.320); currency transaction
reports on cash over $10,000 in a day (31 C.F.R. § 1010.311); customer
identification and recordkeeping, with the transmittal-of-funds and travel rules
at $3,000 (31 C.F.R. § 1010.410); five-year record retention; and sanctions
screening against the lists administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control,
which binds everyone regardless of registration status. Structuring transactions
to avoid any of these reports is itself an offence under 31 U.S.C. § 5324.

Note also that a religious organisation carrying on a trade or business that
receives more than $10,000 in cash in one transaction or related transactions
must report it on Form 8300 (26 U.S.C. § 6050I; 31 U.S.C. § 5331).

## 8. The rule the Kingdom operates under

Three sentences, and this system is built to enforce them.

**Internal accounts are pure receivable and payable book-keeping.** They record
what a member has given and what the Kingdom owes that member. They hold no
member funds, bear no interest, are not redeemable on demand, and cannot be
transferred to another member. There is no member-to-member transfer function
here and none will be added.

**Every actual payment moves through a regulated institution in the Kingdom's own
name.** A bank account held by the Kingdom, cheques and transfers drawn on it,
under the segregation-of-duties controls in the treasury document. Nobody holds
Kingdom money in a personal account, and the Kingdom holds nobody else's.

**Any proposal to move value between members goes to counsel before it goes to
anyone else.** It is a licensing question with a compliance budget attached and a
felony statute behind it, not a feature request, and the answer is nearly always
that a licensed provider already does it better and cheaper. The Kingdom's
strength is its record, not its rails.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual, prepared to organise the Kingdom's affairs and keep
its officers on defensible ground. It is not legal advice, and no attorney-client
relationship arises from it or from anything else in this system. Money
transmission, banking, and Bank Secrecy Act questions turn on the precise
mechanics of the particular arrangement and on every jurisdiction touched by it,
and the regulations and thresholds change. Engage licensed Connecticut counsel
with financial services experience before accepting, holding, or moving anyone
else's money.
