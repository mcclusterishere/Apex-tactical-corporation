# What We Actually Are

*For the Founder, personally. You asked whether the documents help for anything,
whether this is an established entity, and whether signing and notarising the
Charter accomplished something — and you said plainly that you do not want to
register with anybody and want this to remain an unregistered, unincorporated
religious body. That constraint is taken as fixed. This memorandum works inside
it and tells you what the unregistered form actually gives you, exactly where it
costs you, and what can be done about each cost without a filing. Where a filing
is genuinely the only fix, it is said once and then left alone.*

---

## 1. The answer to the question you asked

**Yes. Apex Kingdom is a real legal form, it is a named category in the
Connecticut statutes, and you did not have to file anything with anyone to be
it.** Conn. Gen. Stat. § 33-264a provides, in full: *"Three or more persons
uniting for public worship may form a corporation or a voluntary association.
Such a corporation shall be called a religious corporation and such a voluntary
association shall be called a religious society."* An unincorporated religious
body in Connecticut is not a grey area, a workaround, or a thing you have to
argue for. It has a statutory name — **religious society** — and it has had one
since 1969. Connecticut has protected unincorporated religious bodies far
longer than that: § 47-2, still on the books and still operative, dates from
1684.

**And yes, executing and notarising the Charter accomplished a specific and
substantial thing, in four places at once.** Section 33-264c(b) provides that
*"A religious society may, in its charter or by bylaw or otherwise, adopt
provisions relative to its membership, affairs and government."* The statute
does not merely tolerate a charter; it names the charter as the governing
instrument of exactly the kind of body you constituted. Federally, Treas. Reg.
§ 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(2) defines "articles of organization" to include *"the
articles of association, or any other written instrument by which an
organization is created"* — your Charter is the right species of document for
the church tax position, without incorporation. In a property or leadership
dispute, *Episcopal Church in the Diocese of Connecticut v. Gauss*, 302 Conn.
408 (2011), commits Connecticut courts to neutral principles of law, under
which a court reads the deeds, the local church charter, the state statutes on
church property, and any general-church constitution. Yours is the local church
charter. And *Jones v. Wolf*, 443 U.S. 595, 606 (1979), says the arrangement
must be made *"before the dispute erupts"* and *"embodied in some legally
cognizable form."* You did that in May 2025, before any dispute existed. That
part cannot be retrofitted and most bodies in your position never do it.

**Now the honest half.** "Established entity" runs two different questions
together, and the conflation is what hurts people. As a **religious society**
and as a **charitable trust**, you exist now, with no filing, and that status is
real. As a **legal person wholly separate from you and your members for
liability purposes**, you are not there, and no drafting gets you there. There
are also two open questions sitting underneath everything below, and you should
carry them from here on: first, § 33-264a's gate is *three or more persons
uniting for public worship*, which is a question of fact about your community,
not a question of drafting; second, § 33-264b says a society formed under
§ 33-264a *"shall file"* a certificate with the Secretary of the State, and
whether the powers in § 33-264c are available to a society that never filed is
**unresolved in Connecticut, with no case law either way**. Section 5 of this
memorandum returns to both.

## 2. What you already hold, without filing anything

This is the part nobody has laid out for you, so it is laid out at length. Each
item is what it is good for, not merely what it is.

### (a) A statutory form with a name — § 33-264a

*Good for:* answering the question "what are you?" in four words that a lawyer,
an underwriter, an assessor, or a judge will recognise. "An unincorporated
religious society under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 33-264a" is a sentence that ends an
argument. "A sovereign body politic" is a sentence that starts one. The
statutory category is the single most useful thing in this memorandum because
everything else attaches to it.

### (b) Power to hold and convey property in the society's own name — § 33-264c(a)

Verbatim: a religious society *"may hold, manage, acquire, purchase, sell,
convey and have and exercise any rights of ownership in real and personal
property for the use and support of public worship or for any ecclesiastical,
missionary, charitable or educational purpose. Grants, donations, devises and
bequests of real and personal property may be received and held in trust or
otherwise for such purposes."*

*Good for:* defeating the standard objection. The general Connecticut rule is
that a voluntary association cannot hold real estate as such — the CGA's own
annotation to § 52-76 says so flatly: *"Voluntary association cannot, as such,
hold real estate. 44 C. 260."* Section 33-264c(a) is a specific override for
religious societies. As a matter of law you can take title unincorporated. That
is unusual and it is worth knowing you have it. (As a matter of title-company
practice it is harder; see § 6(b). Do not let anyone collapse the two.)

### (c) Governance entirely by your own instrument — § 33-264c(b) and § 33-264k

Section 33-264c(b) lets the society set its "membership, affairs and government"
by charter. Section 33-264k directs that the whole part *"shall be liberally
construed in the interests of religion and morality."*

*Good for:* everything internal. Titles, offices, succession, membership,
discipline, the Sovereign Head structure itself. Connecticut does not tell you
how to organise a religious society and expressly instructs its courts to read
the chapter in religion's favour. Liberal-construction clauses are rare in the
General Statutes and this one is pointed at you.

### (d) A statutory shield for members against creditors — § 33-264c(d)

Verbatim: *"A member of a religious society or of a religious corporation shall
not be liable for any obligation to the creditors of such religious society or
religious corporation."*

*Good for:* the thing everyone assumes only incorporation supplies. Connecticut
has no Uniform Unincorporated Nonprofit Association Act — I checked the chapter
tables of Title 33 and Title 34 myself; there is no such chapter. For a generic
association, § 52-76 expressly authorises suits *"against such an association
and its members."* Section 33-264c(d) is a flat statutory answer to that, for
religious societies specifically, with no incorporation required.

Three limits, stated now so you do not over-rely on it. It protects *members*
against *creditors*; it does not on its face address a trustee or officer acting
for the society. **Whether it defeats a tort judgment creditor is an open
question — a tort plaintiff is a creditor after judgment, but § 52-76 expressly
permits tort actions against members, and no Connecticut court has reconciled
the two. Unconfirmed, and it matters.** And its availability depends on the
§ 33-264b filing question in § 5.

### (e) Capacity to sue and be sued in the Kingdom's own name — § 52-76

*Good for:* a caption. "Apex Kingdom" can appear on a writ as plaintiff or
defendant without naming every member. The Connecticut Supreme Court in *Benoit
v. Amalgamated Local 299*, 150 Conn. 266 (1963), described the statute as
procedural — it creates no substantive right. It gives you standing to appear
under your own name, which is not nothing, and it is not a shield.

### (f) Capacity to take gifts, devises and bequests for religious use — § 47-2

The 1684 statute, verbatim: *"All estates granted for the maintenance of the
ministry of the gospel, or of schools of learning, or for the relief of the
poor... or for any other public and charitable use, shall forever remain to the
uses to which they were granted, according to the true intent and meaning of the
grantor, and to no other use whatever."* The CGA's printed annotations record:
*"Bequest to unincorporated charitable institution sustained; 17 C. 181; and
also a devise. 98 C. 333."*

*Good for:* accepting a legacy. An unincorporated religious body in Connecticut
can be left property in a will and take it. Note the sting in the tail — "to no
other use whatever." Property taken this way is held *for a purpose*, not owned
outright.

### (g) A charitable trust, created by declaration, registered nowhere

Conn. Gen. Stat. § 45a-499v(2) creates a trust by *"declaration by the owner of
property that the owner holds identifiable property as trustee."* Section
45a-499w(e) is Connecticut's own addition with no counterpart in the uniform
act: *"A charitable trust is created if the donor makes a gift with a charitable
intent."* Section 45a-499z(a) names *"the advancement of education or religion"*
as a charitable purpose. Section 45a-499bb says a trust need not be written at
all, and expressly excepts charitable trusts from the clear-and-convincing
standard that applies to other oral trusts.

**There is no charitable trust registry in Connecticut.** I read the section
list of Chapter 802c — 230 sections — and there is no registration, filing, or
recording provision. Chapter 598a (§§ 33-281b, 33-281c) also governs charitable
trusts and imposes no registration either. This is a real difference from
California, whose Gov. Code § 12581 sweeps in *"all charitable corporations,
unincorporated associations, trustees, and other legal entities holding property
for charitable purposes."* If you have read that charitable trusts must register
with the Attorney General, you were reading about another state.

*Good for:* holding property permanently for the Kingdom's purposes, with no
filing anywhere, and with a document — the certification of trust under
§ 45a-499zzz — that lets you prove authority to a bank or a title company
*without handing over the Charter*. Section 45a-499zzz(d) says the certification
*"need not contain the dispositive terms of a trust,"* and (h) makes a person who
demands the whole instrument in bad faith liable for damages and fees. There is
no equivalent instrument for an unincorporated association. This asymmetry is
written into the statute and it is the main practical reason to hold property
through a trust rather than in the association's bare name.

### (h) Federal tax exemption with no application — 26 U.S.C. § 508(c)(1)(A)

Section 508(a) makes an organisation give notice — file Form 1023 — before it is
treated as described in § 501(c)(3). Section 508(c)(1), headed **"Mandatory
exceptions,"** switches that off for *"churches, their integrated auxiliaries,
and conventions or associations of churches."* IRS Publication 1828: *"Churches
that meet the requirements of IRC Section 501(c)(3) are automatically considered
tax exempt and are not required to apply for and obtain recognition of tax-exempt
status from the IRS."*

And 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(3) provides that *"the term 'corporation' includes
associations"* — which is why an unincorporated body qualifies under § 501(c)(3)
and § 170(c)(2) at all. **Incorporation is required for neither exemption nor
donor deductibility.** That misconception costs unincorporated churches money
every year.

*Good for:* exactly what it says, and it is the cleanest "you already have it"
in this memorandum. Read `docs/01-LEGAL-POSTURE.md` § 3(b) alongside it: § 508 is
a filing exception, not a grant of status, and it relaxes none of § 501(c)(3)'s
substantive rules.

### (i) No annual return, and no possibility of automatic revocation

26 U.S.C. § 6033(a)(3)(A)(i) excepts churches from the annual information
return. No Form 990. And the auto-revocation provision, § 6033(j), reaches only
organisations *"described in subsection (a)(1) or (i)"* — churches are excepted
out of (a)(1), and (i) covers only the small-receipts categories. **A church
cannot be auto-revoked for non-filing because it has no filing obligation to
fail.** "We filed nothing for fifteen years" ends most nonprofits and does not,
by itself, end a church. Note the period is **three** consecutive years for
organisations that do have the obligation, not two.

### (j) Deductible contributions, with no determination letter and no listing

Section 170(c)(2) defines a qualifying donee by its *characteristics* — organised
and operated for religious purposes, no inurement, no campaign intervention.
Nothing in § 170(c) conditions the deduction on IRS recognition. Churches are
separately named in § 170(b)(1)(A)(i). The IRS says so operationally in its
Tax Exempt Organization Search FAQ: *"Some donees (i.e., churches, group ruling
subordinates, and governmental units) eligible to receive tax-deductible
charitable contributions may not be listed in Pub. 78 Data."*

*Good for:* telling a donor the truth. The catch is on their side, not yours:
under § 170(f)(8) no deduction is allowed for a gift of $250 or more without a
contemporaneous written acknowledgment from you, and Pub. 1828 is blunt that a
church which fails to acknowledge *"incurs no penalty; but without a written
acknowledgment, the donor can't claim a tax deduction."* The penalty for your
paperwork failure falls entirely on the person who gave you money.

### (k) Statutory restrictions on IRS church inquiries — 26 U.S.C. § 7611

An "appropriate high-level Treasury official" must form a reasonable written
belief before an inquiry; notice requirements apply; and § 7611(c)(1)(A) requires
the Service to complete an examination within two years of the notice date. And
the definition in § 7611(h)(1) reaches *"any organization claiming to be a
church"* — the protection attaches to the claim, not to proven status.

*Good for:* more than it looks. In *United States v. Living Word Christian
Center* (D. Minn. 2009) a court refused to enforce a summons because the officer
who authorised the inquiry was not high-level enough after the 1998 IRS
restructuring abolished the regional tier the statute names. **Unconfirmed:** the
IRS proposed regulations to fix this in 2009 and, on the Unified Agenda entry
last checked, they were still not final. Treat the protection as live and
contested rather than settled.

### (l) Property tax exemption written to include unincorporated bodies

Four subdivisions of § 12-81 matter, and the drafting is deliberate:

- **(12)** — personal property *"owned by, or held in trust for, a Connecticut
  religious organization, **whether or not incorporated**."* The words are in the
  statute.
- **(13)** — *"houses of religious worship, the land on which they stand, their
  pews, furniture and equipment owned by, or held in trust for the use of, any
  religious organization."*
- **(14)** — real property and equipment held in trust for a religious
  organisation used as a school, parish house, camp, or similar.
- **(15)** — the parsonage.

Contrast subdivision **(7)**, the general charitable exemption, which is drafted
in terms of a *"corporation"*, is subject to §§ 12-87 and 12-87a, and carries its
own quadrennial statement requirement. Subdivisions (13), (14) and (15) are
subject to § 12-88 only. Sections 12-87 and 12-87a reach subdivisions (7), (10),
(11) and (16) — **not (12) through (15)**.

*Good for:* real money, claimed under the right subdivision. Claim under
(13)/(14)/(15) **by number, in writing**, to the assessor. The writing matters
independently: § 12-89(c) grants appeal rights to *"any taxpayer or organization
filing a tax-exempt statement or application for exemption,"* so a claim you
never made in writing may be a denial you cannot appeal. For assessment years
from 1 October 2022, § 12-89(a) requires the board to state its rationale on the
record and § 12-89(b) requires written notice of denial with appeal rights.

**One trap, and it is the one an earlier reading of this got wrong.** Section
**12-89a** provides that an organisation claiming exemption under any of
subdivisions *"(7), (8), (10), (11), (12), (13), (14), (15), (16), (18), (27),
(29), (49) or (58)"* *"may be required upon request, at any time"* by the
assessor to submit an IRS certification of exemption. Subdivisions (12) through
(15) are all listed. **So an assessor's demand for an IRS determination letter is
a statutory entitlement, not local custom** — and a church relying on automatic
§ 508(c)(1)(A) exemption has no letter to produce. Do not write to an assessor
saying no IRS letter is authorised; you would be wrong on a provision they handle
routinely, and you would spend your credibility on your weakest point. Section
12-89a is permissive and does not on its face make certification a condition of
exemption, and § 12-81(12)–(15) condition exemption on religious ownership and
use rather than federal certification. That is the argument. Be ready to make it,
and ready to appeal.

### (m) Personal immunity for uncompensated officers — § 52-557m and the federal VPA

Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-557m immunises a director, officer or trustee of a
nonprofit *"qualified as a tax-exempt organization under Section 501(c),"* who is
*"not compensated for such services on a salary or prorated equivalent basis,"*
for acts in the exercise of *"policy or decision-making responsibilities,"* in
good faith and within scope, *"unless such damage or injury was caused by the
reckless, wilful or wanton misconduct of such person."*

Note the word **qualified**, not *recognised*. On the better textual reading the
immunity is available without an IRS determination letter. **Unconfirmed — no
Connecticut case construes that phrase**, and the immunity in any event requires
that the body genuinely satisfy § 501(c)(3), including no private inurement. It
also protects the *person*, not the body, and covers governance decisions, not
operational negligence.

The federal Volunteer Protection Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 14501–14505, protects
volunteers of a nonprofit organised for religious purposes — § 14505(4) requires
no IRS determination and no incorporation on that prong — for those receiving
under $500 a year, excepting wilful or criminal misconduct, gross negligence,
reckless misconduct, and motor vehicle operation. **Connecticut has not opted out
under § 14502(b)** (New Hampshire is the state that did). *High confidence from
secondary sources; the negative was not verified against a session-law search.*

### (n) Two federal regimes that do not reach you *because* you are unincorporated

- **The Corporate Transparency Act.** 31 U.S.C. § 5336(a)(11)(A) defines a
  reporting company as an entity *"created by the filing of a document with a
  secretary of state or a similar office."* A common-law trust and a common-law
  association are neither. That structural point holds regardless of the state of
  FinCEN's rulemaking.
- **Bank beneficial-ownership certification.** 31 C.F.R. § 1010.230(e)(1) defines
  a "legal entity customer" the same way. You are not one, so no beneficial
  ownership form is owed by law.

*Good for:* a genuine, quantifiable advantage of the form you have chosen. Note
also the flip side, flagged and unresolved: **if you were ever to file the
§ 33-264b certificate, whether that makes the society an entity "created by the
filing of a public document with a Secretary of State" is unsettled and I found
no authority either way.** Textually it should not, because § 33-264a creates the
society and the certificate comes afterward. But it is a real cost to weigh, and
it is not obvious.

### (o) A court that will read your Charter — *Gauss*, 302 Conn. 408 (2011)

The Connecticut Supreme Court held that *"the neutral principles of law approach
is preferable because it provides the parties with a more level playing field."*
The method examines the deeds, the local church charter, the state statutes
governing church property, and the general church's constitution. You have no
general church and no denominational canons overhead, so of those four
categories the operative document is yours.

Understand what that cuts both ways on. *Gauss* applied neutral principles and
ruled **for** the hierarchical church, because the Dennis Canon put an express
trust in the documents. Neutral principles is a method, not a thumb on the scale
for local charters. It enforces whatever the paper says — which is why § 7(f)
below insists the Charter must not be silent.

### (p) Exemption from charitable solicitation registration — § 21a-190d(1)

*"Any duly organized religious corporation, institution or society"* is the first
listed exemption from §§ 21a-190b and 21a-190c — that is, from annual
registration, the $50 application fee, the $25-per-month late fee, and the annual
financial reports and audit thresholds. There is no dollar cap on it and no
renewal. It is a substantial benefit. **It is also conditional on a filing, and
that is § 8 of this memorandum.**

### And the price of all of it, which nobody told you

Two sections of the same chapter are the cost of the shield, and they are aimed
squarely at a founder who treats the body's property as his own. Both verified
verbatim.

**§ 33-264e (Dissolution).** After liabilities, assets go *"only to another
religious society, religious corporation or other organization formed for public
worship or for ecclesiastical, missionary, charitable or educational purposes."*
The section ends: *"No property of a religious society or religious corporation
shall be distributed to its members."*

**§ 33-264f (Judicial dissolution).** Where a society *"has failed, for two years
or more, to hold religious services and to maintain its organization,"* any
member, any interested person, **or the Attorney General** may petition the
Superior Court to wind it up. And: *"No property of a religious society or a
religious corporation shall be distributed among its members, or appropriated by
any person for private use, and any person appropriating or endeavoring to
appropriate, for private use, the assets of any such organization shall be deemed
guilty of and subject to the penalties provided for larceny."* If the Attorney
General receives information of such an attempt, *"he shall forthwith petition
the superior court."*

Read that plainly. **Once assets are in a Connecticut religious society they are
not yours.** Taking them for private use is not a governance dispute and not a
tax problem — the statute assigns it the penalties for larceny and makes Attorney
General action mandatory on information. Nothing in the Charter and nothing in
the words "Sovereign Head" displaces it. Federal law converges on the same point
from the other side: the § 501(c)(3) no-inurement rule, which Pub. 1828 says
reaches *"insiders (such as persons having a personal and private interest in the
activities of the organization). Insiders could include the minister, church
board members, officers."*

This is the standard bargain for every charitable form in every state, and it is
not a reason to avoid the religious society. But decide that you accept it before
you put personal assets in, and before you take anyone else's money. See
`docs/08-TREASURY-AND-FINANCIAL-CONTROL.md` and `docs/12-ASSETS-AND-THE-
POPULATION-REGISTER.md` § 10 on how the controls actually work.

## 3. What the notarisation did, and what it did not do

**What it did.** It fixed a date and authenticated a signature. That converted a
proof problem into a document. If someone later disputes when the Charter came
into existence, whether you signed it, or whether it was written after a dispute
arose rather than before, you hold an executed original with a notary's seal
instead of testimony. Under *Jones v. Wolf* the timing is the whole point: the
arrangement must be made *"before the dispute erupts."* A notarised instrument
dated May 2025 proves that in a way a recollection never will. It also satisfies
the ordinary formality expectations of banks, title companies, and underwriters,
who ask for an executed governing document and are relieved when one exists.

**What it did not do.** It conferred no status. A notary attests that the person
who appeared before her signed the document; she does not certify that the
document is legally effective, that the entity it describes exists, that its
recitals are true, or that any government has recognised anything. There is no
sense in which notarisation registers, charters, files, or approves anything.

**And the specific gap that matters most.** Section 33-264c(b) speaks of
provisions the *society* adopts. A Charter executed, witnessed and notarised by
one person is excellent evidence of that person's intent. It is not, on its face,
a charter **adopted by a society of three or more persons uniting for public
worship**, which is what § 33-264a requires. The notarisation authenticates your
signature; it does not authenticate the existence of a congregation.

If three or more people have in fact united for worship, have them adopt the
Charter at a warned meeting and minute it, with a signed membership roll. That
costs nothing, requires no registration, and is **the single highest-value
document you do not currently have**. It is first on the plan in § 10 for that
reason.

## 4. The Citation

The Official Citation of the Connecticut General Assembly, dated 5 October 2025,
introduced by nine legislators and signed by the President Pro Tempore, the
Speaker, and the Secretary of the State, is a genuine honour and creditable
evidence that public officials thought the Kingdom's contribution to cultural
heritage, education, and community empowerment worth marking on the record. It is
worth having, worth displaying, and worth using with the people it opens doors
with. Nine legislators know the Kingdom's name; `docs/04-GOVERNMENT-RELATIONS-
PROTOCOL.md` exists to work that relationship properly.

**It is not state recognition. It confers no legal status, creates no entity,
grants no exemption, and establishes no government-to-government relationship of
any kind.** The State says so in its own words: the official portal page for
legislative citations states that these documents *"are a courtesy to the
requester, are strictly honorary and are not legally binding."*

The contrast is exact and it is in your own file. When the Connecticut General
Assembly actually creates an entity, it does it by Special Act, and the text reads
like Special Act 05-5 (2005): *"There is created a body politic and corporate of
perpetual duration to be known as The Connecticut Supreme Court Historical
Society."* That took both chambers and the Governor's signature. A citation
signed by nine legislators is a different instrument doing a different job,
however distinguished the signatories.

The practical rule is the one already in `docs/01-LEGAL-POSTURE.md` § 3(d) and it
does not change here. Presented honestly — as recognition of contribution — it is
a real credential that opens real doors. Presented to a bank officer, an
assessor, a title examiner, or a judge as evidence of legal status, it destroys
your credibility in the moment credibility is the only asset in the room, and it
taints everything filed alongside it. Do not place it in a document where a
reader could take it as a grant of status.

## 5. The right words

You do not need a lecture on this; you need the vocabulary. Here it is.

### "Body politic" means a governmental corporation created by legislative act

Conn. Gen. Stat. § 8-244(a): *"There is created a body politic and corporate to
be known as the 'Connecticut Housing Finance Authority'. Said authority is
constituted a public instrumentality and political subdivision of this state and
the exercise by the authority of the powers conferred by this chapter shall be
deemed and held to be the performance of an essential public and governmental
function."*

*There is created.* The legislature creates it. Public instrumentality, political
subdivision, governmental function — a body politic is a piece of the State's own
machinery. Special Act 05-5, quoted above, is the same formula. An association
calling itself a body politic does not become one, and the cost is not
prosecution: the cost is that the instant a bank officer, an assessor, or
opposing counsel reads "body politic" on your charter, they classify the whole
document and stop reading. You lose the benefit of every sound provision in the
same instrument — and every provision in §§ 2 and 7 of this memorandum is a sound
provision.

### Say these instead

- **"an unincorporated religious society organized under the laws of the State of
  Connecticut"**
- **"a religious society within the meaning of Conn. Gen. Stat. § 33-264a"**
- **"an unincorporated voluntary association"**
- **"a charitable trust for religious purposes"** (for the property side)

All four are accurate, all four are stronger than "body politic," and all four
cost nothing.

### "Religious society" is a defined term — use it deliberately

This one is specific to you. "Religious society" is not a lay phrase in
Connecticut; it is the statutory name in § 33-264a. Using it either (a) claims
§ 33-264a status, in which case § 33-264b's filing duty attaches on its face, or
(b) is being used loosely, in which case it claims no statutory powers and gets
none. Decide which you mean and say so in the instrument. If you want § 33-264a
status, claim it expressly. If you do not, use "religious body" or "ecclesiastical
body" and disclaim formation under Chapter 598 in terms. Ambiguity here gets
resolved by somebody else, later, against you.

### Ecclesiastical titles are yours; civil governmental status is not

"Kingdom," "Sovereign Head," "Crown," a court of the Kingdom sitting on internal
discipline — all of that is internal governance, expressly protected by
§ 33-264c(b) and by the religious autonomy doctrine described in
`docs/01-LEGAL-POSTURE.md` § 3(a). The line to hold is between **describing
internal governance**, which is protected, and **asserting civil governmental
status**, which is not yours to assert. Everything on the internal side is safe.
Nothing on the external side is.

### *Nunc pro tunc* is history, not jurisdiction

The Charter recites effectiveness back to 30 October 2010. Among the people who
signed it, that recital is honest and binding and records when the community
considers itself to have begun. It does not create legal existence in 2010, does
not give you a 2010 chain of title, does not backdate a tax position, and is not
evidence against a third party who dealt with you in 2015. Used internally, it is
fine. Used externally as a jurisdictional claim, it is the same category of error
as "body politic."

It also cannot reach the one place it would be worth reaching. Section 33-264j(a)
grandfathers each religious society *"established as a voluntary association under
part I of this chapter prior to October 1, 1969,"* which holds all the rights of
a § 33-264a society *"without further filing with the Secretary of the State."*
2010 is not before 1969. There is no route to that grandfather.

## 6. Where the unregistered form genuinely costs you

Four costs, in order of how much they can take from you.

### (a) Liability — and this is the one that matters

**The mechanism, stated exactly.** Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-76 provides that *"Civil
actions may be brought, both in contract and tort, against such an association
and its members,"* subject only to a joinder requirement satisfied in one line of
a caption. Section 52-292 then provides that association property is taken first,
and thereafter *"the property of any member of such association against whom
judgment was rendered jointly with such association may be taken upon execution."*
Its final sentence: *"Nothing herein contained shall be construed as prohibiting
the plaintiff in any action of tort from satisfying such judgment out of the real
estate of such association."*

Section 33-264c(d) is the answer to this — for members, for creditor obligations,
if it applies. Whether it reaches a tort judgment creditor is unresolved. Whether
it applies at all to a society that never filed the § 33-264b certificate is
unresolved. **Two unresolved questions stacked on each other is not a plan.**

**The thing that actually hurts, and it is counterintuitive.** Connecticut
apportions negligence liability severally under § 52-572h(c). But § 52-572h(g)
provides that on the claimant's motion, after good-faith collection efforts, the
court *"shall determine whether all or part of a defendant's proportionate share
of the recoverable economic damages and recoverable noneconomic damages is
uncollectible from that party, and shall reallocate such uncollectible amount
among the other defendants."*

**An association with no assets and no insurance does not absorb a judgment. It
exports it onto the individuals named alongside it.** The asset-lessness that
feels like protection is the precise condition that triggers reallocation. This
is the single most important sentence in this section.

**The scenario.** A visitor falls on a stair at a gathering. Counsel sues Apex
Kingdom, its trustees, and you personally. Service is on you — § 52-57(e) permits
service on the presiding officer, secretary or treasurer, and if none resides in
Connecticut, the Secretary of the State is deemed appointed as agent. Execution
runs the same way under § 52-365. The association has nothing. Its share is
reallocated. You are a named defendant, and you were also the person who set up
the staging.

**Where the common law lands if § 33-264c(d) is unavailable.** *Azzolina v. Order
of the Sons of Italy*, 119 Conn. 681 (1935), holds that members of a nonprofit
association are *not* partners and are liable only on agency principles — *"only
those members who authorize or subsequently ratify an obligation are liable on
account of it."* The court found error in a lump judgment and directed judgment
against each participating defendant for his aliquot share; of 170 members, 68
who had not participated owed nothing. That is at least as much a limiting case
as a cautionary one. But the opinion also speaks of authorising an obligation
*"by becoming or remaining a member knowing that such a contract would be
reasonable and proper,"* and **whether that reaches an ordinary member for an
ordinary lease or utility bill is an open question in Connecticut, not a settled
rule.**

**Stated once, plainly: incorporation is the only thing that removes derivative
liability** — exposure that attaches to a person *because* he is a member or
officer. Section 52-76 and § 52-292 create that pathway and a nonstock or
religious corporation closes it.

**And here is why that matters less for you than it sounds.** Incorporation does
not shield anyone from liability for torts he personally commits. Not a
corporation, not an LLC, not a trust. In a body where the founder runs the
events, the *direct* liability path is the likely one and the *derivative* path
is the unlikely one — so for Apex Kingdom the practical value of incorporating is
smaller than it first appears, and the practical value of insurance is
correspondingly larger. If you had incorporated believing it solved your tort
exposure, you would have bought the wrong thing and possibly skipped the right
one. That is the honest accounting, and it is the end of the subject.

### (b) Property

**The law is on your side; the practice is not, and the two get conflated
constantly.** Section 33-264c(a) gives an unincorporated religious society power
to take title in its own name. Title insurers and lenders will nonetheless
resist, because they underwrite a promise about the future and they need to know
who has authority to convey, how that authority is evidenced of record, and who
signs. With no statement of authority available in Connecticut (see § 7(e)) and
no case law construing § 33-264c, expect an underwriter to want a filed
certificate, a certified vote, an attorney's opinion, or trustee vesting — or to
decline. **This is a title-marketability problem, not a legal-capacity problem.
Say that distinction out loud to the closing agent.**

**Unconfirmed:** I could not verify the Connecticut Standards of Title, which the
Connecticut Bar Association sells and does not publish. Treat this paragraph as
informed expectation and have a Connecticut conveyancing lawyer confirm before
you buy real property.

**The § 47-20 trap, which is specific and expensive.** Section 47-20 provides
that the word *"trustee"* following a grantee's name, *"without any other language
expressly limiting the powers, interest or estate of the grantee,"* does not, *"in
the absence of a separate duly executed and recorded instrument defining the
powers of the grantee, affect the right of the grantee to sell, mortgage or
otherwise dispose of the real estate."* A deed to "M. McCluster, Trustee" gives a
future trustee, on the face of the land records, unrestricted power to sell — and
protects the buyer against the trust's claim. The Attorney General's remedy runs
against the trustee personally; it does not recover the land. The fix is in the
statute's own escape clause and is in § 7(d).

**Succession is the practical failure point.** An instrument naming trustees with
no self-executing succession mechanism clouds title on every death, resignation
and turnover. That is what title companies actually balk at — not the absence of
a corporate charter.

### (c) Banking

**Separate the law from the policy, because it changes the conversation.**

- **Law, binding on the bank:** 31 C.F.R. § 1020.220(a)(2)(i)(A)(4)(i) requires a
  taxpayer identification number for a U.S. person. That is why you need an EIN.
  Non-negotiable. (Subparagraph (B) permits opening for a customer who has applied
  for but not yet received a TIN.)
- **Not law:** § 1020.220(a)(2)(ii)(A) says a bank's programme *"must contain
  procedures that set forth the documents that the bank will use. These documents
  **may include**"* — and then lists, *"such as,"* certified articles of
  incorporation, a business licence, a partnership agreement, **or trust
  instrument**. "May include" and "such as." The list is illustrative. And
  § 1020.220(a)(2)(ii)(B) permits non-documentary verification entirely.

So when a bank says "federal law requires a state formation certificate," that is
wrong. Their onboarding checklist is a risk-based document *they* wrote. Nothing
in the regulation forbids opening an account for an unincorporated religious
society, and the beneficial-ownership rule does not reach you at all (§ 2(n)).
Note the practical asymmetry, though: **a trust instrument is named in the
regulation's example list and an association charter is not**, which is why
compliance departments clear the former faster. Expect friction, expect to shop
institutions, and expect community banks and credit unions to be materially more
flexible than national banks.

### (d) Insurance, sales tax, and grants

**Insurance.** Carriers will write an unincorporated religious body; nothing in a
standard commercial general liability form conditions coverage on corporate
status. Some carriers' underwriting nonetheless asks entity type and a few
decline unincorporated risks outright, and some D&O forms use corporate-assumption
wording in the definition of "Insured Organization" that needs endorsing. Because
your members' protection rests on § 33-264c(d) — a shield with no interpreting
case law in fifty-six years — rather than on a corporate shell, **liability
coverage is carrying more load for you than it would for an incorporated church.**

**Sales and use tax — the one clean, quantifiable cost, and it is statutory.**
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 12-412(8) exempts sales to an organisation that is exempt
under § 501(a) *"and that the United States Treasury Department has expressly
determined, by letter, to be an organization that is described in Section
501(c)(3) or (13),"* and then requires presentation of the determination letter
plus a CERT-119, or a pre-1 July 1995 Connecticut exemption permit. The State no
longer issues those permits. **There is no route for a new organisation without a
federal determination letter. You pay Connecticut sales tax on everything you
buy, indefinitely, as a matter of statute.**

One partial offset that is available to you today with no filing of any kind:
**§ 12-412(94)** exempts *"Sales of tangible personal property by nonprofit
organizations at bazaars, fairs, picnics, tag sales or similar events to the
extent of five such events of a day's duration held during any calendar year."*
No determination letter appears in that text. Up to five one-day fundraising
events a year, sales exempt. Keep a dated log. **Unconfirmed:** whether DRS reads
"nonprofit organizations" in (94) to import subdivision (8)'s letter requirement.
Nothing in the text does; confirm with DRS before the sixth event.

**Grantmakers.** Institutional and community foundations almost uniformly require
a determination letter or a verified public record, because their own compliance
depends on documenting the grantee; the alternative is expenditure responsibility,
which most decline to run. Institutional grant funding is largely closed to you
without a letter. This is private-party practice, not law.

**And the thing worth knowing before you rule it out:** a determination letter is
available to an unincorporated body. Nothing in § 501(c)(3), § 7701(a)(3), or
Treas. Reg. § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(2) requires an applicant to be incorporated. Form
1023 is a **federal** filing; it registers you with no state, incorporates
nothing, and leaves your unincorporated status untouched. One federal form would
answer the sales tax problem, the § 12-89a problem, and the grantmaker problem
together. Whether you want it is your call and a coherent one either way — but the
choice is genuinely severable from the incorporation question, and almost
everybody is told otherwise.

## 7. What you can do about all of that without registering anything

Every item here requires no filing with any state agency.

### (a) Insurance — by a wide margin the highest-leverage action available

Not a consolation prize. One instrument covers the body, its officers, its
employees, its volunteers, and — with ISO form **CG 20 02, "Additional Insured —
Club Members"** — its members, and it comes with a **duty to defend**, which is
often worth more than the indemnity because it stops a meritless claim consuming
you in fees before anyone reaches the merits. No statute does that.

In priority order:

1. **Commercial general liability.** $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate is the
   market standard for congregations.
2. **Workers' compensation the moment anyone is paid anything.** Section
   31-275(10) defines "employer" to include a *"voluntary association"*, with no
   religious exemption. And § 31-284(a) confers the exclusive-remedy shield only
   on *"An employer who complies with the requirements of subsection (b)"* — **no
   coverage means no exclusive remedy**, so the injured worker sues in tort at
   large. Section 31-288(c) makes a civil penalty mandatory on a finding of
   non-compliance, and § 31-288(d) adds $100 per day up to $50,000, plus stop-work
   authority. This is non-negotiable and it requires no incorporation, only a
   policy.
3. **Sexual abuse and molestation coverage.** Frequently sublimited or excluded
   from a base CGL and must be asked for by name. Where minors are present this is
   the catastrophic exposure and the one that most often exceeds limits and reaches
   individuals.
4. **Directors and officers.** CGL does not answer a governance claim — an
   expelled member, an employment claim, a breach-of-duty allegation in a schism —
   because those are not bodily injury or property damage. Your structure
   concentrates governance in one person, so **the governance claim is your most
   likely claim and CGL will not touch it.** Confirm in writing that the policy's
   "Insured Organization" definition reaches an unincorporated association.
5. **Property**, if the Kingdom owns anything.

Underwriters will ask for the governing instrument, a roster of officers, average
attendance, activities, premises, whether anyone is paid, whether there is youth
programming, vehicle use, and claims history. **Your Charter answers most of that
file, and most small congregations cannot produce one.** It is an asset here.

**Unconfirmed:** pricing. Public aggregators put small-congregation general
liability roughly in the $400–$1,800 a year range. That is marketing data, not
underwriting data. Get three quotes from church specialists and one from a
nonprofit specialist for the D&O; the real number is knowable in a week.

### (b) Contract discipline — free, and it is where the money is actually lost

- **Sign in a representative capacity, always.** *"Matthew McCluster, Presiding
  Officer, for Apex Kingdom, an unincorporated religious society, and not
  individually."* An officer who signs in his own name is liable on his own
  signature and § 33-264c(d) never enters the analysis.
- **Refuse the personal guaranty.** No commercial landlord leases to a body with
  no balance sheet without asking for one. This is universal underwriting
  practice, not a legal requirement. **The moment you sign a guaranty, every
  protection in this memorandum becomes irrelevant to that lease** — you are
  liable in your own name, and it happened at signing, not at breach. Offer a
  larger security deposit instead.
- **Put a limited-recourse clause in every lease and vendor contract.** *"Recourse
  under this agreement is limited to the assets of the Society."* It binds the
  party who signed it, by ordinary privity, and it costs nothing. It does nothing
  against a tort plaintiff who never signed anything, which is the entire category
  in § 6(a).

### (c) Indemnification in the Charter — worth doing, and weak on its own

An indemnity is a promise by the association to pay, only as good as the
association's assets, and the person most likely to need indemnifying is the
person who controls whether it is paid — a conflict a court will notice.
**Indemnification plus D&O is real protection, because the insurer funds the
promise.** The clause is the plumbing; the policy is the water. It cannot lawfully
cover the reckless, wilful or wanton conduct § 52-557m carves out, and it cannot
cover a § 33-264f private-appropriation claim.

### (d) A trust for property, plus the recorded instrument that makes it work

Hold real property through a **common-law trust**: named trustees hold legal title
for the society, under an instrument specifying appointment, succession, removal,
and the conditions on conveying. This is expressly contemplated by § 33-264c(a)
(*"in trust or otherwise"*), by § 52-292 (*"whether held by it or by trustees for
its benefit"*), and by § 47-6 (*"the trustees of any voluntary association"*).
**A common-law trust requires no filing with anyone.** Do not use a Chapter 615
statutory trust (§§ 34-500 et seq.) — those *are* created by filing with the
Secretary of the State, which is both a registration and the thing that could
convert you into a CTA reporting company.

**Then record, in the town land records, a separate duly executed instrument
expressly defining and limiting the trustees' powers.** That is § 47-20's own
escape clause, and without it the words "as trustee" on a deed protect a *buyer*
from a rogue trustee rather than protecting the land from him. This is a
**recording**, not a registration: it goes to the same town clerk as the deed,
there is no state agency, no review, and no entry in any registry of
organisations.

Two honest limits. Section 52-292 reaches association property *"whether held by
it or by trustees for its benefit,"* so **a trust does not shield property from a
judgment against the association**; its value is title marketability and
continuity of ownership. And § 47-2 locks the use forever — anyone who tells you a
trust makes the property judgment-proof or recoverable to you personally is
selling something.

### (e) A statement of authority — Connecticut does not have one, and here is what to use instead

You should know this because it is a real gap and it nearly was not one. The
Uniform Unincorporated Nonprofit Association Act would have given Connecticut a
recordable **statement of authority**, indexed by the town clerk, naming who may
convey the association's property and making that authority conclusive in favour
of a person who gives value without notice. That was § 6 of Raised Bill No. 1357
in the 2005 session. **It never became law.** I read the bill status page myself:
the bill was reported out as a Joint Favorable Substitute, and what the Governor
signed on 7 June 2005 was **Special Act 05-5, "AN ACT INCORPORATING THE
CONNECTICUT SUPREME COURT HISTORICAL SOCIETY."** The bill number was reused as a
vehicle for an unrelated subject, and a Special Act is not codified into the
General Statutes in any event. Connecticut had balked once before: the Law
Revision Commission voted in December 1998 to send the Act back for further
study, members having expressed concerns about extending liability protection to
members of unincorporated associations and about letting informal associations
hold and transfer property in the association's name. *(That 1998 record is
corroborated in substance but I could not load it from a primary source — the CGA
page errors and the Internet Archive is blocked from this network. Treat it as
reliable background, not as a citation.)*

Search engines will tell you Connecticut adopted the Act in 2005. **It did not.**
Anyone saying otherwise is reading a bill number, not a law.

**What to use in its place, all of it filing-free:**

1. **A certification of trust under § 45a-499zzz** for anything held in trust.
   This is Connecticut's actual statutory instrument for proving authority without
   disclosing terms, and it has teeth — (f) and (g) protect a person who relies on
   it in good faith, and (h) makes a bad-faith demand for the whole instrument
   compensable in damages and fees. It is the closest thing Connecticut has to a
   statement of authority and it exists today.
2. **A signed, minuted resolution** designating who may sign, open accounts,
   contract, and convey — with a certificate of incumbency the presiding officer
   signs. This is what banks and closing agents are actually asking for.
3. **The recorded trustee-powers instrument** in § 7(d), for land.

### (f) Fix the Charter's silences — free, and the highest-value drafting you will ever do

*Gauss* means a Connecticut court will read this document. Make it worth reading.
Six things it must not be silent about:

- **Who holds title, in what capacity.**
- **Who determines membership**, and how membership is granted, suspended, ended.
- **How the Sovereign Head is succeeded**, by whom, on what event.
- **Whether and how the Head can be removed** — *and if the answer is "not at
  all," say so expressly*, because silence invites a court to supply a rule.
- **What happens to property on schism**, expressly, with a reverter or trust in
  favour of the body continuing under the Charter.
- **That amendment requires the Head's assent.**

*Jones v. Wolf* blessed a state's presumptive rule of majority representation as
consistent with the First Amendment. **Unconfirmed — I found no Connecticut
authority establishing a majority-rule default for an independent religious
society**, so the accurate warning is not "silence means majority rule" but
something almost as bad: if the Charter is silent, a court has no instrument to
enforce and must fashion a rule, and majority representation is the rule the
Supreme Court has blessed and the most available one to reach for. For a Sovereign
Head structure, a faction that recruits more members than you have would then take
the property.

**Two more Charter provisions, each one paragraph, each closing a live hole:**

- **Enforcement standing under § 45a-499z(c).** Connecticut's version of the
  Uniform Trust Code reads: *"The settlor of a charitable trust, or a person
  designated by the settlor who would not otherwise have standing, may maintain a
  proceeding to enforce the trust **only to the extent specified in the trust
  instrument**."* Compare the uniform text, UTC § 405(c): *"The settlor of a
  charitable trust, among others, may maintain a proceeding to enforce the
  trust."* **Connecticut deleted the settlor's default standing.** So as things
  stand, if a successor officer breaches, *you cannot sue to enforce your own
  trust* — and neither can your members: *Derblom v. Archdiocese of Hartford*, 346
  Conn. 333 (2023), dismissed the donor's executrix, eleven former students, their
  parents, and a successor operator, all for want of standing, on the rule that the
  Attorney General has exclusive authority. Connecticut wrote you a door and it
  only opens from inside the instrument. Name yourself. Name a successor. Name a
  council. Section 45a-499dd(c) works the same way for modification.
- **An express dissolution clause** consistent with § 33-264e. Treas. Reg.
  § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(4) allows the dedication requirement to be met by a provision
  in the articles *"or by operation of law"*, and § 33-264e supplies it — but that
  route depends on a predicate an examiner could contest. The express clause costs
  one paragraph and moots the issue. Read § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(i) at the same
  time: the articles must limit purposes to exempt purposes **and** must *"not
  expressly empower the organization to engage, otherwise than as an insubstantial
  part of its activities, in activities which in themselves are not in
  furtherance of"* exempt purposes. A grand, open-ended powers clause — exactly
  what a founding charter naturally contains — fails that second prong on the face
  of the document, with no investigation of conduct required.

### (g) Waivers — use them, do not rely on them

Connecticut is one of the hardest states in the country for exculpatory
agreements. *Hyson v. White Water Mountain Resorts of Conn., Inc.*, 265 Conn. 636
(2003): a release does not bar a negligence claim absent language expressly so
providing. *Hanks v. Powder Ridge Restaurant Corp.*, 276 Conn. 314 (2005): even a
clear and unambiguous release of prospective ordinary negligence may violate
public policy, on a totality-of-the-circumstances analysis. *Reardon v. Windswept
Farm, LLC*, 280 Conn. 153 (2006), applied *Hanks* to horseback riding and
invalidated the release.

The distinction that may help: *Hanks* and *Reardon* both involved a paying
customer of a commercial recreation business under an adhesion contract. A church
gathering with no fee and no adhesion contract sits outside the core of that
reasoning. **Unconfirmed — no Connecticut appellate decision I located applies
*Hanks* to a nonprofit religious setting.** Genuinely open.

Draft a participation agreement that expressly names negligence, recites specific
identified risks, is signed voluntarily with a genuine alternative to signing, and
is **not a condition of access to worship**. Its real value is evidentiary — it
proves the participant was warned, which supports comparative negligence under
§ 52-572h(b), where a plaintiff more than 50% at fault recovers nothing.

**Minors: treat a parent's pre-injury waiver as unenforceable for planning
purposes.** The Connecticut Supreme Court has never decided the question and
Superior Court decisions conflict. For youth activity the protection is insurance
and supervision ratios, not paper. And no release anywhere excuses reckless,
wilful or wanton misconduct — that limit runs through § 52-557m, § 52-557h, and
42 U.S.C. § 14503(a) alike.

### (h) Establish the three persons, get an EIN, and never commingle

- **Three or more persons uniting for public worship** is the gate on all of
  Chapter 598, and § 33-264f keeps it open afterwards: two years without religious
  services exposes you to judicial dissolution on the Attorney General's petition.
  Membership roll, organisational minutes, adherent signatures on the Charter,
  dated service records. Free. Do it first.
- **An EIN** (Form SS-4, with the "Church or church-controlled organization" box
  at line 9a) is a taxpayer number, not a registration, not incorporation, and not
  exemption. You cannot bank or issue a W-2 without one. One caution: the IRS
  states that when you apply it *"presumes the organization is legally formed,"*
  which starts a filing clock, and EIN-holders with no returns do get swept into
  automatic-revocation notices even though a church cannot lawfully be revoked for
  non-filing. Keep § 2(i) of this memorandum with the EIN letter so the notice can
  be answered.
- **A dedicated bank account, never commingled.** This protects you more than any
  filing. Commingling is what destroys these structures — it is the fact pattern
  that turns a governance dispute into a § 33-264f larceny referral, it defeats the
  "distinct legal existence" factor the IRS lists first among its church criteria,
  and it forfeits the § 501(c) qualification that § 52-557m immunity depends on.
  One habit costs you all three at once.
- **Contemporaneous evidence of assembly.** Under *American Guidance Foundation v.
  United States*, 490 F. Supp. 304 (D.D.C. 1980), and the IRS's application of it,
  the associational test is what actually decides church status: *"At a minimum, a
  church includes a body of believers or communicants that assemble regularly in
  order to worship."* *Spiritual Outreach Society v. Commissioner*, 927 F.2d 335
  (8th Cir. 1991), held that regular bi-monthly gospel programmes were not enough,
  because nothing indicated the participants considered it *their church*. Service
  dates, attendance, orders of worship, minutes. Free, requires no filing, and it
  is the whole ballgame on examination.

## 8. The one registration that may attach whether you like it or not

**Charitable solicitation.** Read this section carefully; it is the only place in
this memorandum where doing nothing creates real exposure.

**What triggers it.** Section 21a-190b(a): *"Every charitable organization not
exempted by section 21a-190d shall annually register with the department prior to
conducting any solicitation."* "Solicit" is defined about as broadly as language
permits — § 21a-190a(3) reaches *"any request directly or indirectly for money,
credit, property, financial assistance or other thing of any kind or value,"*
expressly including any oral or written request, any announcement to the press or
by broadcast, and any published handbill or advertisement, and it closes: *"A
solicitation shall be deemed to have taken place whether or not the person making
the same receives any contribution."* Asking counts. Receiving nothing does not
save you. And § 21a-190a(2) defines "person" to include a *"trust, foundation or
any other entity however styled"* — **being unincorporated is not a defence; the
definition was drafted to reach every form.**

**A donate button counts, and Connecticut says so.** The Department of Consumer
Protection's published FAQ: *"The State of Connecticut has not legislatively
adopted the Charleston Principles, but we do abide by them,"* and *"if an
organization is located in CT and only solicits via the internet (donate button on
website) it needs to register in CT."* You are located in Connecticut.

**Does the religious exemption save you? Yes — from registration and financial
reporting. Not from a filing.** Section 21a-190d, verbatim in its lead-in:

> *"The following charitable organizations that engage in solicitation shall not
> be subject to the provisions of sections 21a-190b and 21a-190c, **provided each
> such organization, prior to conducting any solicitation or prior to having any
> solicitation conducted on behalf of others, shall submit such information as the
> department may require to substantiate an exemption under this section in a form
> prescribed by the commissioner**: (1) Any duly organized religious corporation,
> institution or society;"*

The proviso is not decorative and the legislature tightened it deliberately. The
history line on the section records that P.A. 10-9 *"added provision requiring
information to be submitted in form prescribed by commissioner,"* effective 5 May
2010, and P.A. 19-177 *"added 'that engage in solicitation' and ', prior to
conducting any solicitation or prior to having any solicitation conducted on
behalf of others,'"*, effective 9 July 2019. It was moved from a passive status to
an affirmative pre-solicitation duty seven years ago, on purpose.

Connecticut sits in the middle. New York's Exec. Law § 172-a(1) and California's
Gov. Code § 12583 both exempt religious organisations self-executingly, with no
filing. Connecticut conditions the exemption on submitting substantiating
information first.

**What the filing actually is.** The legacy paper form is CPC-54, "Claim of
Exemption from Registration"; the current path runs through DCP's online licensing
system. **No fee.** One time, not annual — DCP's own summary says *"Exemption need
be claimed only once and does not have to be renewed."* No financial reporting.
It asks the organisation's legal name, address, website, email, EIN, purpose and
programmes, where and when the organisation was established, IRS status (where
"not exempt" is an offered answer with no adverse consequence), and which
exemption is claimed — the first box on the list reads *"A duly organized
religious corporation, religious institution or religious society."*

Two cautions. The form instructs that *"Registration must be approved by the
Department of Consumer Protection prior to solicitation,"* which asks for more
than the statute's "submit" — **build in lead time; do not file on a Friday and
launch a donate button on Monday.** And the word *"duly organized"* in subdivision
(1) is **undefined in the Act**. In practice DCP appears to accept a self-declared
religious body and ask for more if it wants more; that is administrative practice,
not law, and it can change. **Unconfirmed:** I could find no reported Connecticut
decision construing § 21a-190d — the whole chapter has generated a single
annotated citation, *State v. Ebenstein*, 219 Conn. 384 (1991), under § 21a-190c.
Absence of reported decisions is not the same as no court ever having considered
it; unreported Superior Court decisions are not comprehensively searchable.

**What the exemption does *not* reach, which nobody mentions.** Section 21a-190d
exempts you from §§ 21a-190b and 21a-190c only. It does not touch § 21a-190h
(prohibited acts), § 21a-190i (investigative subpoenas), or § 21a-190l
(enforcement). Three prohibitions bind you today, exemption or no exemption:
§ 21a-190h(3), engaging in a financial transaction unrelated to the charitable
purpose or jeopardising the organisation's ability to accomplish it;
§ 21a-190h(4), expending an unreasonable amount on solicitation or management; and
§ 21a-190h(14), *"any person to appropriate any property of a charitable
organization for a private use."*

**And the shape of the downside is not a fine.** Section 21a-190l(c) is criminal
and requires knowledge; § 21a-190l(d)'s civil penalty requires wilfulness. But
§ 21a-190l(b) is not penal at all and requires no scienter: the Attorney General
may apply to the Superior Court for an injunction, *"the appointment of a
receiver, an order of restitution, an accounting"* and other relief, *"brought in
the name of the state."* **For a body whose entire point is not being administered
by the State, a receiver over its funds is the worst available outcome, and it is
reachable from a failure to submit a free one-page form.**

**The asymmetry, stated once.** Filing the exemption claim costs nothing, recurs
never, and creates a state record of a name you already published in a legislative
citation signed by the Secretary of the State. Not filing it, while soliciting,
exposes you to an accounting and a receiver. There is no version of this where not
filing is the better trade — **unless you do not solicit at all, which is a
legitimate and fully available choice.** If Apex Kingdom is funded by the Founder,
by unsolicited gifts, or by genuine governing-member dues, the Act never engages
and nothing is owed.

That last route is real and worth designing for. Section 21a-190a(5) provides that
*"'Contribution' shall not include bona fide fees, dues or assessments paid by
members, provided membership is not conferred solely as consideration for making a
contribution,"* and § 21a-190a(12) defines "membership" as that which entitles a
person to the organisation's privileges *"and the rights to vote, elect officers
and hold office."* **If your members genuinely govern, their dues are not
contributions.** That sits awkwardly with a Sovereign Head model in which members
do not elect officers, and you cannot have the dues treatment without the
governance being real. It is a structural choice, and it is yours to make.

## 9. What is not known, stated plainly

The credibility of this library rests on this list being honest.

1. **Whether the § 33-264b certificate is constitutive.** Nothing in Chapter 598
   voids an unfiled society, imposes a penalty, or conditions the § 33-264c powers
   on filing — which favours you. But § 33-264j(a) excuses filing *"without further
   filing with the Secretary of the State"* **only** for societies established
   before 1 October 1969, and the ordinary negative inference is that everyone else
   must file to hold "all the rights, powers, privileges and immunities." That is
   *expressio unius* and it is the argument the other side will make. **No
   Connecticut decision construes § 33-264a, § 33-264b, or § 33-264c** — there is
   not a single case annotation under § 33-264c in fifty-six years. This is the
   first question for counsel, because the answer determines whether § 33-264c(a)
   and (d) are available to you at all.
2. **Whether § 33-264c(d) reaches a tort judgment creditor** or only a contract
   creditor. The words fit a contract creditor comfortably; § 52-76 expressly
   permits tort actions against members. No authority reconciles them.
3. **Whether *Azzolina*'s "remaining a member" language reaches an ordinary member
   for ordinary contracts.** The holding does not decide it.
4. **Whether "duly organized" in § 21a-190d(1) requires organisation under a
   particular Connecticut statute.** Undefined in the Act, unconstrued by any
   court.
5. **Whether § 52-557m's "qualified as" reaches an organisation with no IRS
   determination letter.** Textually the better reading; no Connecticut case.
6. **Whether Apex Kingdom is a "church" for federal purposes**, as opposed to a
   religious organisation that must file Form 1023 within 27 months and file Form
   990 annually. This is a facts question turning on regular assembly of an
   identifiable body who regard it as *their church*, and it governs the entire
   federal position in §§ 2(h)–(k). I have no facts to apply and cannot assess it.
7. **Whether Apex Kingdom presently has three or more persons uniting for public
   worship.** Same character of question; it governs all of § 2(a)–(d).
8. **The current § 33-264b filing fee.** The Secretary of the State's fee schedule
   and the form CICR-1-1.0 both state $50, one time, with no annual report and no
   renewal, but the live business.ct.gov page currently lists only a Certificate of
   Amendment at $20 and does not list the formation certificate. Confirm with the
   Commercial Recording Division before assuming anything.
9. **Whether filing that certificate would make the society a "legal entity
   customer" under 31 C.F.R. § 1010.230(e)(1)**, or a reporting company under
   31 U.S.C. § 5336(a)(11)(A). Probably not, textually; no authority either way.
10. **Connecticut title practice.** The Connecticut Standards of Title are sold by
    the CBA and not public. Section 6(b) is informed expectation, not verified fact.
11. **Whether *Hanks* applies to noncommercial religious settings**, and **whether
    a parent may waive a minor's claims** — Superior Court decisions conflict and
    the Supreme Court has never decided it.
12. **The 1998 Law Revision Commission report** on UUNAA — corroborated in
    substance, not loaded from a primary source.
13. **Insurance pricing**, and whether particular D&O forms reach an unincorporated
    association without endorsement. Confirm against the actual policy form before
    binding.
14. **Whether the § 7611 regulations were ever finalised.** Projected, not final,
    on the last agenda entry checked.
15. **Whether DRS reads § 12-412(94)'s "nonprofit organizations" to import
    subdivision (8)'s determination-letter test.** Nothing in the text does.

Two things that are **not** on this list, because they are settled and were
verified directly: Connecticut has **not** adopted UUNAA or RUUNAA — 2005 SB 1357
was gutted and signed as Special Act 05-5 incorporating the Connecticut Supreme
Court Historical Society, and the chapter tables of Titles 33 and 34 contain no
such act. And 224 Conn. 797, cited in the Chapter 598 annotations, is *Rector,
Wardens & Vestrymen of Trinity-St. Michael's Parish, Inc. v. Episcopal Church in
the Diocese of Connecticut*, 620 A.2d 1280 (1993).

## 10. What to actually do, cheapest and highest-value first

Nothing in items 1 through 9 requires registering with anybody.

1. **Establish three or more persons on paper, today.** Membership roll,
   organisational minutes of a warned meeting adopting the Charter, adherent
   signatures. Free. It is the gate on all of Chapter 598 and the one thing the
   current paperwork does not show.
2. **Start a dated worship log** — service dates, attendance, order of worship.
   Free. It is what proves church status under the associational test and it
   forecloses § 33-264f's two-year trigger. It cannot be reconstructed later.
3. **Get an EIN** and **open a dedicated account. Never commingle.** No filing,
   no status conferred, and it protects you more than anything else on this list.
4. **Amend the Charter.** One sitting, no cost: enforcement standing under
   § 45a-499z(c) and § 45a-499dd(c); succession; removal (or express
   irremovability); membership; property on schism; amendment requiring the Head's
   assent; an express § 33-264e-consistent dissolution clause; and a purposes
   clause that satisfies both prongs of Treas. Reg. § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(i).
5. **Change the vocabulary.** Strike "body politic." Adopt "unincorporated
   religious society under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 33-264a." Keep *nunc pro tunc* as
   internal history and never as a jurisdictional claim. Free, and it protects
   every other provision in the same instrument from being read past.
6. **Get insurance quotes this month.** GL with abuse coverage named
   specifically, D&O confirmed in writing to reach an unincorporated association,
   and workers' compensation the moment anyone is paid. This is the item standing
   between a bad afternoon and your household, and the **limits** matter more than
   any other number in this library.
7. **Adopt the contract discipline in § 7(b)** — representative-capacity
   signature block, no personal guaranty, limited-recourse clause in every lease
   and vendor agreement. Free.
8. **If you will solicit anything, including a donate button: file the DCP claim
   of exemption first and allow time for approval.** Free, one-time, no renewal,
   no financial reporting. If you will not solicit, file nothing and owe nothing.
9. **Before acquiring real property**, draft the declaration of trust with
   self-executing successor appointment, and record the § 47-20 trustee-powers
   instrument in the town land records. A recording, not a registration.
10. **Take six narrow questions to Connecticut counsel.** They are narrow enough
    to be cheap: (i) does § 33-264c(d) reach a society that never filed the
    § 33-264b certificate, given § 33-264j(a); (ii) does it bar a direct tort claim
    against a trustee, or only creditor claims; (iii) does § 12-89a's IRS-letter
    request operate as a condition of exemption under § 12-81(13)–(15) or only as
    an evidentiary demand; (iv) does "duly organized" in § 21a-190d(1) require a
    state filing; (v) confirm the current CICR-1 fee with the Commercial Recording
    Division; (vi) read the Charter against Treas. Reg. § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(i)
    and (b)(4).
11. **Then, and only then, price the two optional filings — knowing exactly what
    each buys and that neither is incorporation.**
    - **The § 33-264b certificate.** A one-page form, no annual report, no
      renewal, no registered agent, no bylaws or members disclosed. It does not
      create a corporation — § 33-264d exists precisely because becoming one is a
      separate, later, elective act by two-thirds vote on a different form. What it
      buys is that § 33-264c(d), the most valuable provision in this memorandum,
      stops being an argument and becomes a fact. If your objection is to corporate
      form, this does not violate it. If your objection is to the State holding a
      piece of paper with your name on it, it does, and that is a coherent position
      — hold it knowing that § 33-264j(a) makes the unfiled reading weaker than you
      may have been told, and that no court has ever ruled either way.
    - **Form 1023.** A **federal** filing. It incorporates nothing, registers you
      with no state, and an unincorporated association can hold a determination
      letter. It buys the § 12-412(8) sales tax exemption, the answer to § 12-89a
      on property tax, and access to institutional grantmakers. If your purchasing
      volume is meaningful, it is the highest-value action available inside your
      stated constraint.
