# The Rights Enforcement Playbook

*For whoever holds the office of Counsel. Read it in full on the day you are
commissioned, before sending anything to an outside party. Thereafter read
section 4 before every enforcement decision and section 7 before every second one.
No legal training is assumed; none of what follows requires it.*

## 1. What the Kingdom owns, and how to find out

The Kingdom's intellectual property is the only part of its position that binds
strangers. A copyright is good against someone who has never heard of the Charter
and would laugh at its jurisdiction. That is why this office exists, and why its
first duty is an inventory rather than a letter. Enter every item in the Register
of Rights: the Charter and later instruments; sermons; curricula; liturgies; songs
and recordings; the seal, logos, and devices; names, titles of office, and
slogans; photographs; video; every page of every website and social account; and
the software behind this register.

**Copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium**
(17 U.S.C. § 102(a)) — sermons, curricula, songs, recordings, photographs,
artwork, web copy. A sermon nobody records is not fixed and is not protected, so
record everything, and with the preacher's authority, because fixation must occur
by or under the authority of the author (17 U.S.C. § 101). Protection then arises
automatically; what must be filed is what makes it worth suing on.

**Three things copyright does not protect.** Names, titles, and short phrases —
37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a) says so expressly, so "Apex Kingdom" and every motto are
trademark questions and nothing else. Ideas, systems, methods, and concepts
(17 U.S.C. § 102(b)): the governance model and the ceremonial sequence are free
for anyone to take, while the words expressing them are protected. And facts.
Counsel must be able to state that distinction in a sentence, because most
enforcement mistakes are made by people who cannot.

**Ownership is settled first, and it is rarely what people assume.** Under
17 U.S.C. § 204(a) a transfer of copyright ownership is invalid without a writing
signed by the owner; there is no oral assignment and no implied one. The volunteer
who designed the seal owns the artwork, the contractor owns the website code, the
musician owns the hymn. Work-made-for-hire does not rescue this — it reaches
employees acting within the scope of employment, and otherwise only nine
enumerated categories of commissioned work under a signed agreement (17 U.S.C.
§ 101; *Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid*, 490 U.S. 730 (1989)). So for
every item: name the author, name the owner, record whether a signed assignment
exists, and where none does, obtain it while the relationship is warm.

## 2. Copyright registration, done properly

Registration runs through eCO at copyright.gov. An application has three parts and
is not complete until all three arrive: form, fee, and deposit copy.

The **Single Application** is cheapest and strictly limited — one work, one author
who is also sole claimant, not made for hire — so little here qualifies; the
**Standard Application** covers the rest. Several **group options** are worth real
money: unpublished works in groups of up to ten by one author in one
administrative class; short online literary works published within a three-month
window in groups up to fifty; photographs up to seven hundred and fifty per
application; and separate options for serials and newsletters. The **deposit** is a
complete copy of the work as filed, uploaded electronically for most categories.

Two dates matter and they are not the same. The **effective date of registration**
is the day the Office received the last of application, deposit, and fee in
acceptable form (17 U.S.C. § 410(d)). The **certificate** arrives months later
bearing that later date, but the registration relates back. Record the effective
date; it decides everything below.

**Section 411(a): registration is a precondition to suit.** No infringement action
on a U.S. work may be instituted until registration has been made, and in *Fourth
Estate Publishing Ass'n v. Wall-Street.com, LLC*, 586 U.S. 296 (2019), the Supreme
Court held this requires the Register to have acted on the application, not merely
received it. An unregistered work is a work the Kingdom cannot sue on.

**Section 412: the three-month window.** Statutory damages and attorney's fees are
unavailable for infringement of an unpublished work commenced before the effective
date of registration, and for infringement of a published work commenced after
publication and before registration — unless registration was made within three
months of first publication.

That is worth a great deal. With it, 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) allows statutory damages of
$750 to $30,000 per work, rising to $150,000 for wilful infringement, with no need
to prove a dollar of actual loss, and § 505 permits an award of attorney's fees.
Without it the Kingdom must prove actual damages — for a sermon copied onto a
stranger's blog, approximately nothing — and pays its own lawyer. That is the
difference between a claim a competent attorney will take on contingency and a
claim no attorney will take at all, and registration within three months of
publication costs under a hundred dollars.

## 3. Trademark, done properly

A trademark identifies source. Rights arise from **use in commerce** — from
actually offering services under the name — and registration under the Lanham Act
(15 U.S.C. § 1051 et seq.) adds nationwide constructive notice, a presumption of
validity, and the full remedial scheme.

**Distinctiveness governs strength.** Generic terms are never protectable,
descriptive terms only on proof of secondary meaning, and suggestive, arbitrary,
and fanciful marks are inherently distinctive (*Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting
World, Inc.*, 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976)). Prefer a coined or arbitrary mark
whenever the Kingdom names something new.

**Clear the mark before filing.** Search the USPTO register, state registers,
business name databases, domains, and the ordinary web for confusingly similar
marks in related services. Skipping it is expensive: fees are not refundable, so a
likelihood-of-confusion refusal costs the whole fee plus the response, and in the
worse case the Kingdom has printed the mark on everything and hears from the senior
user instead. One caution particular to this institution: 15 U.S.C. § 1052(b) bars
registration of governmental insignia, and a device reading as an official seal
invites refusal for falsely suggesting a government connection.

**Filing basis.** A § 1(a) application rests on use already made and needs a
specimen at filing. A § 1(b) application rests on a bona fide intent to use, holds
priority from the filing date, and requires a Statement of Use with a specimen
after the Notice of Allowance — within six months, extendable to thirty-six, each
extension carrying a fee. Use § 1(b) to reserve a name settled on but not launched.

**Specimens fail more applications than anything else.** For services the specimen
must show the mark used in the sale or advertising of those services *and* show a
direct association with them; a page bearing the mark without reference to what is
offered will be refused, as will mockups and printer's proofs. Screenshots need the
URL and access date, and use of the mark merely as the organisation's name is not
trademark use.

**Classes likely relevant here:** Class 45 for religious and fraternal society
services, Class 41 for education and training, Class 36 for charitable
fundraising. Fees are per class, so file where the Kingdom operates.

**The maintenance calendar is unforgiving.** A § 8 declaration of continued use is
due between the fifth and sixth anniversaries of registration, with a six-month
grace period on payment of a surcharge; thereafter a combined § 8 and § 9 renewal
every ten years. A § 15 declaration of incontestability is optional after five
years of continuous use and worth filing. Miss the § 8 window and the registration
is cancelled — not suspended, not revivable — and the Kingdom refiles as a new
applicant, losing its priority date.

## 4. The escalation ladder

Six rungs. Start at the bottom. Most matters resolve by the third, and an
organisation this size that begins at the sixth will spend its whole annual budget
on one dispute and lose interest in enforcement permanently.

| Rung | Action | Typical cost | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Document and preserve | Nil | Same day |
| 2 | Informal contact | Nil | Days |
| 3 | Platform remedies | Nil | 1–14 days |
| 4 | Formal demand letter | Nil to low hundreds | 2–4 weeks |
| 5 | Administrative proceedings | $1,500–$5,000+ | 2–12 months |
| 6 | Litigation | Five to six figures | 1–3 years |

**(i) Document and preserve.** Full-page screenshots showing URL and timestamp,
page source, an archival capture through a third-party service, and the underlying
file, hashed into the evidence vault with a custody entry. Evidence disappears the
moment the other side is contacted.

**(ii) Informal contact.** A short, courteous message from a named officer. Much
infringement is a member, an admirer, or a small operator who did not know; many
take it down within the hour and some become allies.

**(iii) Platform remedies.** DMCA notices to hosts and search engines, trademark
complaint forms at Meta, Google, YouTube, TikTok, Etsy, Amazon, and eBay, and the
brand protection programmes of the major marketplaces. Free, fast, and effective,
because the platform's own safe harbour depends on responding. Most matters end
here.

**(iv) Formal demand letter.** Section 6. Reserve for parties who ignored rung two
or are plainly commercial.

**(v) Administrative proceedings.** A UDRP complaint transfers a domain registered
and used in bad faith without a lawsuit, at roughly $1,300 to $1,500 for a single
panellist, decided on documents in about two months. Opposition and cancellation go
to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, which can refuse or cancel a registration
but cannot award money or stop use. The Copyright Claims Board (17 U.S.C.
§§ 1501–1511) is a voluntary small-claims tribunal capped at $30,000, usable
without a lawyer, from which respondents may opt out within sixty days.

**(vi) Litigation.** Federal court, because copyright is exclusively federal, at a
cost well into six figures through discovery. Reserve for infringement that is
commercial, substantial, and wilful, by a solvent defendant, on a work registered
in time for § 412 to apply. That conjunction is rare; when it occurs it is worth
pursuing seriously.

## 5. The DMCA notice in detail

A notice satisfying 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3)(A) obliges a service provider to act
expeditiously to remove or disable the material if it wants to keep its safe
harbour. A notice omitting an element does not. All six:

1. A physical or electronic signature of a person authorised to act on behalf of
   the owner of the exclusive right allegedly infringed.
2. Identification of the copyrighted work claimed to have been infringed — or, if
   multiple works at a single site, a representative list.
3. Identification of the infringing material, with information reasonably
   sufficient to permit the provider to locate it. Give exact URLs.
4. Information reasonably sufficient to permit the provider to contact the
   complaining party: address, telephone number, and email where available.
5. A statement that the complaining party has a good faith belief that the use is
   not authorised by the copyright owner, its agent, or **the law**.
6. A statement that the information is accurate, and, **under penalty of
   perjury**, that the complaining party is authorised to act on behalf of the
   owner.

Note precisely what the perjury declaration covers: the sender's authority to act,
not the merits of the claim. State it in statutory form and do not embellish it.

The words "or the law" do work. A knowing material misrepresentation that material
is infringing exposes the sender to damages and fees under § 512(f), and the Ninth
Circuit held in *Lenz v. Universal Music Corp.*, 815 F.3d 1145 (9th Cir. 2016),
that a copyright owner must consider whether the use is a fair use before sending.
So consider it, in writing, and file the consideration in the matter record.

**Counter-notice.** The recipient may serve one under § 512(g)(3), stating under
penalty of perjury a good faith belief that the material was removed through
mistake or misidentification and consenting to federal jurisdiction where they
reside. The provider must then restore the material not less than ten nor more than
fourteen business days later, unless the Kingdom first notifies it that an action
has been filed seeking to restrain the infringing activity. A counter-notice
therefore forces a binary choice: sue within roughly two weeks, or watch the
material return. So do not send a notice on a work the Kingdom would not sue over,
and never on an unregistered work, because § 411(a) means it could not file that
suit if it wished to.

## 6. Demand letters that work

A demand letter is written to be read aloud in a courtroom by opposing counsel with
the sender ten feet away. Write every sentence for that audience and it will work
on the immediate one as well.

Six short parts: who the Kingdom is and what it owns, with the registration number;
what the recipient did, with dates; why that infringes, in two or three sentences;
what the Kingdom demands; by when; and how to reach a named person. Attach the
certificate and the evidence; send by email and certified mail. Demand what the
Kingdom will actually accept — removal, an undertaking not to repeat, transfer of a
domain, an accounting, or a licence. A realistic outcome resolves matters;
demanding capitulation retains a lawyer for the other side.

Four things never appear.

**No threat of criminal prosecution to gain civil advantage.** Connecticut's Rules
of Professional Conduct forbid a lawyer from doing it, and for a lay officer the
exposure is worse, because a demand for money backed by a threat to report a crime
is the fact pattern of extortion.

**No claim the Kingdom cannot substantiate.** Not statutory damages on an
unregistered work, not ownership it has not documented, not a registration it has
merely applied for. Every overstatement is impeachment material.

**No deadline the Kingdom will not enforce.** A demand that expires without
consequence teaches the recipient, correctly, that nothing follows. Set fourteen or
twenty-one days and calendar the follow-up.

**Nothing styled as legal process.** This is the bright line and it does not move.
The letter is correspondence from a rights holder. It is not a summons, a writ, a
notice of default operating as a judgment, a lien, a warrant, or an order; it bears
no seal implying judicial authority and no signature block implying law
enforcement. Internal ecclesiastical discipline among consenting members is a
different thing entirely and is governed elsewhere. Documents imitating court
process directed at outsiders convert a strong civil position into criminal
exposure for the Kingdom's leadership faster than any other act available. Nor may
the letter imply that the signing officer is a licensed attorney if they are not;
holding oneself out as one is an offence under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 51-88.

## 7. When not to enforce

**Fair use is a right, not a loophole.** 17 U.S.C. § 107 directs consideration of
the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial and
whether it serves a further purpose or different character; the nature of the work;
the amount and substantiality used; and the effect on the potential market.
Parody may qualify (*Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994)), and
the first factor asks whether the use substitutes for the original in the same
market (*Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S.
508 (2023)). A scholar quoting the Charter, a journalist reproducing the seal in an
article about the Kingdom, a critic excerpting a sermon to argue with it — all
lawful.

**Nominative use of the mark is lawful.** Anyone may use the Kingdom's name to
refer to the Kingdom: to report on it, review it, criticise it, or say they
attended. Trademark law prohibits confusion as to source, not mention. The Second
Circuit folds this into its ordinary likelihood-of-confusion analysis rather than
applying a separate nominative fair use test (*International Information Systems
Security Certification Consortium, Inc. v. Security University, LLC*, 823 F.3d 153
(2d Cir. 2016)), and 15 U.S.C. § 1115(b)(4) protects descriptive use.

**Commentary and criticism are the cases to leave alone.** Enforcement against a
critic converts a small piece of criticism into a story about an institution
suppressing criticism, and the story reaches thousands the original never would.
Fees shift both ways: 17 U.S.C. § 505 permits an award to a prevailing defendant
(*Kirtsaeng v. John Wiley & Sons, Inc.*, 579 U.S. 197 (2016)), and 15 U.S.C.
§ 1117(a) permits fees in exceptional Lanham Act cases.

**Proportionality.** Answer three questions in the matter file before every
enforcement decision. What does this cost the Kingdom in money, confusion, or
dilution? How many people does it reach? What will pursuing it cost in fees,
officer hours, and attention? An infringement that costs nothing and reaches nobody
is not worth a dispute that costs thousands and reaches everybody. Record the
decision not to enforce, with reasons: documented forbearance is not abandonment,
whereas an undocumented pattern of ignoring infringement is the raw material of a
laches or acquiescence defence.

## 8. Working with counsel

Hand off when the other side has retained a lawyer; a counter-notice has been
served; the amount in issue exceeds what the Kingdom would spend on a lawyer; the
infringement is commercial and continuing; a filing deadline is close; or the
matter touches the Kingdom's status rather than only its rights.

Have this in one folder before the first call and that call costs one hour instead
of five: the registration certificates with effective dates; the chain of title
showing every signed assignment; the evidence capture with its custody record; the
correspondence in date order; a one-page chronology; the constituting instruments;
and a plain statement of the outcome the Kingdom wants.

**Fee arrangements.** Registration work and demand letters are commonly flat-fee
and should be. Contingency exists in IP but requires the three conditions that make
a case worth a lawyer's own capital: a registration timely enough to unlock § 412,
clear wilfulness, and a defendant who can pay. Hybrid arrangements, a reduced
hourly rate against a share of recovery, are the realistic middle. Litigation is
hourly against a retainer. Ask for a written engagement letter with a scope and a
budget.

A lawyer's first weeks on an IP matter are ordinarily spent establishing what the
client owns, when it was created, whether title passed, and whether it was
registered — expensive work, and billed. An institution arriving with certificates,
assignments, hashed evidence, and a contemporaneous ledger has already paid for
itself several times over, and arrives with something better than a good story: a
record an opponent cannot say was assembled after the fact.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual prepared to organise the Kingdom's affairs. It is not
legal advice, and reading it creates no attorney-client relationship. Filing
deadlines, fee schedules, and platform procedures change without notice, and the
matters covered here turn on particular facts and on jurisdiction. Obtain licensed
Connecticut counsel before action is taken.
