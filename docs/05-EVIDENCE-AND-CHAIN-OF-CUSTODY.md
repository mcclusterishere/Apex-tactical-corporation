# Evidence and Chain of Custody

*For Counsel, the Registrar General, and every officer who may be first to see an
infringement, an encroachment, or an incident. Read it before you collect
anything. The first hour decides whether the material is evidence or merely a
printout.*

## 1. What makes evidence usable

Two separate questions must be answered about every item, and confusing them is
the commonest error made by intelligent people handling their own case.

**Authentication.** Under Fed. R. Evid. 901(a) the proponent must produce
evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what they claim it is.
The bar is not high, but it must be cleared for every exhibit. Rule 901(b)(1)
allows a witness with knowledge to say so — the collector testifying, in
substance, *I captured this page on that date and this is the file I captured*.
Rule 901(b)(4) allows authentication by distinctive characteristics. The
Connecticut Code of Evidence imposes a parallel threshold in state court.

Two federal rules added in 2017 shape how this system is built. Fed. R. Evid.
902(13) makes records generated by an electronic process self-authenticating on
the certification of a qualified person, and Fed. R. Evid. 902(14) does the same
for data copied from a device, storage medium, or file where the copy is
authenticated by a process of digital identification — which means hashing.
Together they allow a large body of digital exhibits to be authenticated by
certificate rather than by a witness on a stand, which is what the
content-addressed vault and the hash chain exist to support.

**Hearsay is a separate problem and survives authentication.** An item may be
perfectly authenticated and still inadmissible for the truth of what it says.
Note how often the truth of the contents is not what is offered: in an
infringement matter the page is offered not to prove anything it asserts but
because publishing it was the act complained of, which is not hearsay at all. Be
clear in every matter file about *why* an item is offered.

**The business-records exception.** Fed. R. Evid. 803(6) admits a record made at
or near the time by, or from information transmitted by, someone with knowledge;
kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity; where making it was a
regular practice; shown by a custodian or qualified witness or by certification
under Rule 902(11) or (12) — unless the opponent shows a lack of trustworthiness.

A properly kept register satisfies every element by design: entries made the day
the thing happens, by an officer with knowledge, as the ordinary practice of the
office, in a system recording who entered what and when. Same-day entry is not
fussiness; it is the element the exception turns on. But records created because
litigation is anticipated do not carry the trustworthiness the exception assumes,
and have not since *Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109 (1943). Routine entries
qualify; a memorandum written for the lawsuit does not, and must never be filed
into the register as though it were.

## 2. Collection, by type

**Web captures — four artefacts, always.** A web page is the most common and most
fragile evidence the Kingdom will handle, and it disappears the moment the other
side reads your letter.

1. **A full-page capture showing the URL and the system clock.** Full page, not
   the visible window; print-to-PDF from the browser preserves the URL and date in
   the header and footer. Where the page is long or dynamic, record the screen
   while scrolling through it.
2. **An independent archival capture.** Submit the URL to the Internet Archive's
   Save Page Now, and to a second service where the material matters. This puts a
   dated copy on infrastructure the Kingdom does not control, which is worth more
   than any number of copies it does.
3. **The file itself, with its digest.** Save the page source and assets, compute
   the SHA-256 of what was saved, and deposit it in the vault. Where the material
   is a document, image, or video, save the underlying file, not a picture of it.
4. **A contemporaneous note in the collector's own words.** Who, when, from what
   device and network, how the page was reached, what was visible, anything
   unusual. First person, plain language, no legal conclusions. This is what a
   Rule 901(b)(1) witness reads from years later — worth more than the other three
   if it is honest, and nothing at all if it is polished.

Capture what vanishes with the page: the account handle and its numeric
identifier, the post permalink, the profile page, and the domain registration
record.

**Correspondence.** Export the original message with full headers as a file. A
screenshot of an email is a picture of an email; the headers are where the
evidence lives.

**Physical items and test purchases.** Photograph the parcel before opening. Keep
the packaging, shipping label, receipt, listing capture, and payment record. Do
not use the item.

**Places and premises.** Photograph on a device whose clock is correct, note the
location and direction of each frame, and take wide establishing shots first.

**Witness accounts.** Taken as soon as possible, in the witness's own words,
first person, signed and dated. Do not draft it for them and do not tidy their
phrasing; a statement that sounds like the Kingdom wrote it will be impeached as
a statement the Kingdom wrote.

## 3. Originals and working copies

**The original is never annotated.** Not a highlight, not a marginal note, not a
redaction, not a page number, not a stamp. It is received, hashed, deposited in
the vault, and thereafter touched only to be produced. All work happens on a
working copy, generated from the vault and marked as such on its face; if it is
lost or ruined, another is generated at no cost.

Fed. R. Evid. 1002 requires the original to prove the content of a writing,
recording, or photograph, but Rule 1003 admits a duplicate to the same extent
unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or
admission would be unfair, and Rule 1001(e) treats an accurate electronic copy as
a duplicate. An unaltered original in the vault is what makes those rules work in
the Kingdom's favour rather than against it.

## 4. The custody record

Every movement of an item is an entry, so that anyone — including a hostile
examiner — can account for it from receipt to production with no unexplained gaps.

| Field | What it must contain |
|---|---|
| Item | Identified by its SHA-256 digest, not by filename |
| Action | Received, transferred, examined, copied, produced, returned, or sealed |
| Actor | The named officer performing it, never an office or shared account |
| Counterparty | The named person or organisation on the other side of a transfer |
| Time | Date and time of the act, with time zone — not the time of entry |
| Place | Where the item was held or handed over |
| Purpose | Why the act occurred, in one line |
| Condition | The state of the item, and any anomaly however small |

Record the anomalies — a broken seal, a filename that changed, an unexplained two
days. A custody record with no problems in it reads as one written afterwards; a
record that notes a gap and explains it reads as one kept honestly. And one
officer, one account: a shared login destroys the chain at precisely the point
where it has to name a person.

## 5. Hashing and digital signatures, plainly

A **SHA-256 digest** is a fixed-length fingerprint — sixty-four characters —
computed from a file's contents. The same file always produces the same digest,
changing one byte produces a completely different one, and constructing a
different file with the same digest is, with present knowledge and present
machines, infeasible.

**What a digest proves:** that the file in front of you is byte-for-byte
identical to the file whose digest was recorded. That is integrity, and it is
worth a great deal.

**What it does not prove:** when the file was created, who created it, where it
came from, that its contents are true, or that the web page it captured ever
looked that way. Nor does it prove *when the digest was recorded*, if the only
record of the recording is a log the Kingdom controls. That is the whole reason
for the next section.

A **digital signature** adds identity to integrity, binding a digest to the
holder of a private key so that anyone with the matching public key can confirm
the signature came from that key and the file has not changed since. Its value
equals the value of the key custody behind it: a key three people can reach
proves nothing about which of the three signed. A signature does not date itself
either — that requires a timestamp from a trusted authority under a standard such
as RFC 3161, or an anchor.

## 6. External anchoring

The hash chain proves internal consistency: no entry can be altered without
changing every hash after it. What it cannot prove alone is *age*, because
whoever holds the database could in principle regenerate the whole thing to any
state they liked. An internal log, however elegantly built, is the Kingdom's own
word about its own conduct until something fixes it from outside.

Anchoring is the fix. Publish the chain's head hash somewhere the Kingdom does
not control and every entry beneath it becomes provably older than the
publication. One anchor covers the entire preceding chain, which is why the
operation is cheap and why skipping it is indefensible.

Acceptable anchors, ascending in strength: a certified letter containing the head
hash, sent to counsel and left unopened; publication of the head in the gazette
with a documented distribution list; a timestamp from an RFC 3161 authority; a
public commit or post carrying the head; a notarised declaration of it.

Anchor monthly at minimum, and **immediately** after collecting evidence
connected to any matter the Kingdom may pursue or have to defend. Ten minutes on
the day of collection is the difference between "our records show" and "this was
fixed before you knew we were looking."

## 7. The duty to preserve

The duty attaches when litigation is **reasonably anticipated** — not when a
complaint is filed, not when a lawyer is retained. It commonly attaches before
the Kingdom has decided to do anything at all.

Triggers include sending a demand letter, which fixes the Kingdom's own duty as
surely as the recipient's; receiving a demand, claim, or preservation notice; a
serious injury on the Kingdom's premises; a subpoena or government inquiry; a
member threatening suit; an internal complaint of misconduct; and discovery of an
infringement the Kingdom intends to pursue. From that moment nothing in scope may
be deleted, overwritten, or allowed to expire — including things that delete
themselves.

## 8. Spoliation, in concrete terms

Spoliation is the loss or destruction of evidence a party had a duty to preserve,
and it almost never looks like shredding. It looks like the retention rule that
quietly deletes at ninety days and was never switched off; the phone traded in at
upgrade; the officer who cleaned out an inbox; the security recording that
overwrote itself on a fourteen-day loop while everyone was deciding what to do.

For electronically stored information, Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e) governs. Where ESI
that should have been preserved is lost because a party failed to take reasonable
steps, and it cannot be restored or replaced, the court may order measures no
greater than necessary to cure the prejudice. Only on a finding that the party
acted **with intent to deprive** another of the information may the court presume
the information was unfavourable, instruct the jury that it may or must so
presume, dismiss the action, or enter default judgment.

The adverse-inference instruction, in plain terms, is a judge telling a jury they
may assume the missing evidence would have hurt the party that lost it. It turns
a factual dispute into a credibility verdict, and the party it is given against
loses. Connecticut also recognises intentional spoliation as an independent tort,
*Rizzuto v. Davidson Ladders, Inc.*, 280 Conn. 225 (2007). The sanction is almost
always worse than the evidence would have been.

## 9. Legal holds

**Issue early**, the moment the duty attaches and before anyone has assessed the
merits. There is no cost to a hold that proves unnecessary and no recovery from
one issued a month late.

**Issue broadly.** Define scope by subject matter, not by what seems relevant
today. Name every custodian who might hold anything, and address systems as well
as people: email, messaging applications, phones, cloud storage, the register
itself, physical files, camera systems and their retention loops.

**Say what to do, operationally.** Suspend automatic deletion. Do not delete,
edit, or reorganise anything in scope. Do not replace or wipe devices. If in
doubt, keep it and ask. Acknowledge receipt in writing.

**Reissue periodically**, because a hold issued once is forgotten within a
quarter and staff turnover finishes the job.

**Document the release.** When the matter ends, release in writing with the date,
the reason, and the officer releasing it. An undocumented release looks identical
to an unauthorised deletion.

## 10. The first hour

When an infringement, encroachment, or incident is discovered:

1. **Capture before you contact.** Do not telephone, email, or comment. The
   material disappears within hours of the other side learning you have seen it.
2. **Take the four web artefacts** — full-page capture with URL and clock,
   independent archival capture, the file with its digest, and your own note.
3. **Write the note now, in your own words**, while you remember what you did.
4. **Deposit everything in the vault** and open the custody record.
5. **Open the matter** in the Enforcement Docket and link the evidence to it.
6. **Issue the legal hold**, broadly, before assessing the merits.
7. **Anchor the ledger head** and record where it was anchored.
8. **Check the registration position.** For a work not yet registered, the
   three-month window under 17 U.S.C. § 412 governs whether statutory damages and
   fees will ever be available, and it does not reopen.
9. **Notify Counsel and the Registrar General**, with the matter reference.
10. **Only then decide what to do.** Nothing in the first nine steps commits the
    Kingdom to any course of action, and every one is irrecoverable if skipped.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual prepared to organise the Kingdom's affairs and to
keep its evidence usable. It is not legal advice, and no attorney-client
relationship arises from it. Admissibility, preservation obligations, and
sanctions turn on the particular facts, the forum, and the stage of the matter,
and the rules differ between federal and Connecticut practice. Engage licensed
Connecticut counsel before acting on any of it.
