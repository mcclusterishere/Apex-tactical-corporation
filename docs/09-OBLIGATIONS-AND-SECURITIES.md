# Obligations and Securities

*For the Founder, and for the Treasurer and Counsel who will implement whatever
he decides. Read it before the Kingdom offers, promises, or accepts money against
any instrument carrying a return — a bond, a note, a certificate, a "loan" from a
member, a share in a project — and read it again before the first conversation
with the first prospective subscriber, because that conversation is already an
offer.*

## 1. The question that has to be settled first

There is a widespread belief among religious organisations that the securities
laws are for Wall Street, and that money raised from one's own congregation on
one's own paper for one's own building is a different kind of thing. It is not.
The federal definition of a security is broad, is applied according to economic
reality rather than the label on the document, and has caught church-affiliated
issuers repeatedly. Whether the Kingdom's instrument is a security is a question
with an answer, and the answer is obtained before the instrument exists rather
than after a subscriber asks for their money back.

The good news is real and belongs first: church bonds are a lawful, established
instrument, congregations have financed buildings with them for a century, and
Congress wrote an exemption for exactly this. The Kingdom can use it. What
follows is the price of using it correctly, and the reason organisations that
ignore that price end up before a grand jury believing themselves to have done
nothing wrong.

## 2. What makes an instrument a security

Section 2(a)(1) of the Securities Act of 1933, 15 U.S.C. § 77b(a)(1), defines
"security" to include a note, a bond, a debenture, an evidence of indebtedness,
and an investment contract, among much else. Two lines of authority do the work.

**Notes.** In *Reves v. Ernst & Young*, 494 U.S. 56 (1990), the Supreme Court
adopted the family-resemblance test. Every note is presumed to be a security. The
presumption is rebutted only by showing the note strongly resembles one of a
short judicially recognised list of instruments that are not — consumer financing
paper, a note secured by a home mortgage, a short-term note secured by a lien on
a small business, a character loan to a bank customer, a note formalising an
open-account debt in the ordinary course, and similar commercial paper. The
resemblance is tested on four factors: the motivations of buyer and seller,
investment or commercial; the plan of distribution and whether there is common
trading; the reasonable expectations of the investing public; and whether some
other factor — collateral, insurance, another regulatory regime — reduces the
risk enough to make the securities laws unnecessary.

The facts of *Reves* deserve close attention, because they are almost exactly the
transaction under contemplation. A farmers' cooperative sold uncollateralised,
uninsured demand notes to raise operating capital, offering them to its own
members at an interest rate above what local institutions paid and describing the
arrangement as an investment programme. The cooperative failed, the noteholders
lost their money, and the Court held the notes were securities. A mutual,
member-facing, non-profit issuer raising money from its own community on its own
paper is the paradigm case, not the exception to it.

**Investment contracts.** *SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.*, 328 U.S. 293 (1946), catches
anything not shaped like a note: an investment of money in a common enterprise
with an expectation of profits derived from the efforts of others. That reaches
profit-sharing in a Kingdom enterprise, participations in a property development,
and any scheme in which a member contributes capital and looks to the Kingdom's
work for a return. A fixed promised rate does not take an arrangement outside
*Howey*: *SEC v. Edwards*, 540 U.S. 389 (2004).

**Why "it is just a loan from a member" changes nothing.** The phrase describes
the relationship, not the instrument. The lender parted with money expecting it
back with something added, from an enterprise they do not control. That is the
economic substance the tests were written to capture. Nor does calling the
document a "certificate of participation," a "covenant deposit," or a
"stewardship instrument" help; novel labels attract attention rather than
deflecting it. If the holder expects repayment with a return generated by the
Kingdom's efforts, assume it is a security and proceed from there.

## 3. What section 3(a)(4) actually provides

Section 3(a)(4) of the Securities Act, 15 U.S.C. § 77c(a)(4), exempts any
security issued by a person organised and operated exclusively for religious,
educational, benevolent, fraternal, charitable, or reformatory purposes and not
for pecuniary profit, where no part of the net earnings inures to the benefit of
any person, private stockholder, or individual. That is a genuine exemption and
the foundation on which every legitimate church bond programme in the United
States rests. The Kingdom, on its stated purposes and trust framing, is the kind
of issuer the provision was written for. Mark two conditions inside the text now:
*exclusively* charitable operation, and *no inurement*. An offering that funds an
enterprise operated for profit, or whose economics benefit the Founder or his
household, forfeits the exemption and endangers the tax position described in
`08-TREASURY-AND-FINANCIAL-CONTROL` section 6.

## 4. What the exemption does not do

This is where organisations that read the first half and stopped get into
trouble.

**The exemption is from registration only.** It removes the obligation to file a
registration statement with the Commission before offering, and nothing else. It
is not a licence, not a safe harbour, and not a determination by anyone that the
offering is sound.

**There is no exemption from fraud, ever, for anyone.** Section 17(a) of the
Securities Act, 15 U.S.C. § 77q(a), reaches fraud in the offer or sale of *any*
security, exempt or not; section 10(b) of the Exchange Act, 15 U.S.C. § 78j(b),
and Rule 10b-5, 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5, reach any security registered or not.
Liability attaches not only to lies but to material omissions — failing to
disclose that there are no audited financials, that projected revenue depends on
a congregation that has not yet grown, that an earlier obligation is in arrears,
or that the Founder's household draws compensation from the proceeds. Nor is
scienter required across the board: under *Aaron v. SEC*, 446 U.S. 680 (1980),
sections 17(a)(2) and 17(a)(3) may be violated on negligence alone. Someone
entirely honest who was careless about what they told subscribers is inside that
provision. Connecticut's antifraud provision, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36b-4, has no
charitable exception either.

**The registration violation is strict liability.** Selling an unregistered
security that does not in fact qualify for an exemption violates section 5,
15 U.S.C. § 77e, whether or not anyone intended anything wrong, and gives every
purchaser a statutory right of rescission under section 12(a)(1), 15 U.S.C.
§ 77l(a)(1) — their money back with interest, less income received, subject to
the limitation periods in section 13, 15 U.S.C. § 77m. The burden of establishing
the exemption rests on the person claiming it: *SEC v. Ralston Purina Co.*, 346
U.S. 119 (1953). If the Kingdom's exempt character is contested years later, the
Kingdom must prove it from records made at the time.

**State blue-sky law applies independently.** Federal exemption is not state
exemption. Connecticut's Uniform Securities Act, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36b-2 et
seq., imposes its own registration requirement at § 36b-16 with its own
exemptions at § 36b-21. Whether the Connecticut exemption for a nonprofit
religious issuer is self-executing or conditioned on a notice filing with the
Securities and Business Investments Division of the Department of Banking is a
question to put to that Division in writing, and to keep the answer. Every state
whose resident is offered the instrument applies its own law: one out-of-state
relative of one member, one subscription form mailed across a line, and a second
statute is engaged with a different exemption and a different regulator.

**Anyone compensated for selling may need to register.** Section 3(a)(4) exempts
the *security*, not the *person selling it*. Under section 15(a) of the Exchange
Act, 15 U.S.C. § 78o(a), a person effecting securities transactions for the
account of others generally must register as a broker-dealer or be an associated
person of one, and Connecticut requires registration of broker-dealers and agents
at Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36b-6. Transaction-based compensation — a commission, a
finder's fee, a bonus tied to amounts raised — is the strongest single indicator
of broker status. Never pay anyone a share of what they raise.

**Affinity offerings are a standing enforcement priority.** Unregistered
offerings sold into a congregation on the strength of shared faith are among the
most frequently prosecuted securities matters involving religious organisations,
and the Commission maintains a standing investor alert on affinity fraud. The
reason is structural rather than moral: trust substitutes for diligence, nobody
asks for financial statements, and losses surface late because members are
reluctant to complain about their own institution. Regulators know the pattern,
and an offering that resembles it will be read against it.

## 5. How a legitimate programme is actually run

Church bond programmes that work are conservative, documented, and slow. None of
the components below is an optional flourish; each exists because its absence is
how the failures happened.

An **offering circular** giving full and fair disclosure to the standard of a
prospectus whether or not one is legally required — history and governance,
audited financials, existing debt, the specific use of proceeds, the source of
repayment, the risks in plain terms including total loss, the absence of any
insurance or government guarantee, and the illiquidity of the instrument. An
**independent feasibility assessment** by someone with no interest in the
outcome, testing whether actual giving history supports the proposed debt
service. A **trustee and escrow arrangement**, so subscriptions are impounded
until a stated minimum is raised and returned if it is not, and so an independent
party holds the security and enforces the covenants — an indenture with an
independent trustee is best practice whether or not the Trust Indenture Act of
1939, 15 U.S.C. § 77aaa et seq., is engaged. A **sinking fund** funded on a
schedule from operating revenue, so principal accumulates from the first year
rather than being hoped for in the last. **Security**, ordinarily a first
mortgage, at a conservative loan-to-value. **Projections that are realistic
rather than promotional** — a growth assumption in an offering document is a
representation, and one that was never reasonable is a misstatement. And
**audited financial statements** by an independent accountant, far stronger than
internally prepared books.

## 6. How honest organisations end up prosecuted

The mechanism is consistent enough to be stated as a sequence.

They promise a return they cannot fund — a rate set to be attractive rather than
affordable, above what a bank pays, justified by optimism about growth. They keep
no books that would reveal the shortfall early, so nothing sounds an alarm while
the programme could still be wound down with everyone repaid. Then a maturity
arrives, cash is short, and new subscriptions pay the earlier subscribers.
Everyone involved regards this as a bridge.

That is a Ponzi scheme. It is a Ponzi scheme whatever the officers believed and
whatever they intended to do next month. It is the point at which every statement
to a new subscriber becomes materially false, because a subscriber told their
money will build the sanctuary is not being told it will pay the person who
subscribed last year. The collapse of the Baptist Foundation of Arizona in 1999,
then the largest nonprofit bankruptcy in United States history, followed this
sequence exactly, and the people who ran it were not cynics at the outset.

Hold two things together. Intent is not an element of the registration violation,
so an offering outside the exemption is unlawful whether or not anyone meant
harm. Intent *is* what turns the rest into a criminal fraud prosecution — but
intent is proved from documents, and the document that proves it is the one
showing the officers knew the numbers did not work and offered anyway. The books
that would have saved the institution are the same books whose absence convicts
its leadership.

## 7. What to do instead, in order of preference

Each carries a fraction of the risk, and most are simply better instruments.

**Ordinary bank or credit union borrowing.** A commercial loan is not a security.
The lender does its own diligence, the terms are understood, and no member's
savings are at stake. The underwriting will be searching, and if the Kingdom
cannot satisfy it, that is information the Founder needs before asking members to
lend instead.

**A mortgage secured on church property.** The same, with the security that makes
the rate affordable. Connecticut banks lend to congregations routinely.

**Denominational church-extension lending funds.** Established lenders
specialising in congregational borrowing, with covenants written for churches.
Worth approaching through the Shiloh Baptist affiliation.

**Grants.** Non-repayable, and available in the Kingdom's cultural institution and
heritage capacity as set out in `01-LEGAL-POSTURE` section 3(d). Slow,
competitive, and the cheapest money that exists.

**A capital campaign taking pledges rather than notes.** A pledge is a promise to
give. There is no return, so no investment and no security. This is the
traditional instrument for a building fund and avoids the inurement and solvency
questions entirely. Charitable solicitation registration under Conn. Gen. Stat.
§ 21a-190a et seq. and substantiation under 26 U.S.C. § 170(f)(8) still apply.

**Donor-advised funds.** For donors who want to give but want timing control.
Costs nothing to be ready to receive from one.

If, having read all of this, the Founder still wants to issue, the sequence is:
engage Connecticut securities counsel first and in writing; get the exemption
analysis under both federal and Connecticut law on paper before a single
conversation with a prospective subscriber; commission the feasibility assessment
and the audit before drafting the circular; and record every step in the Register
of Instruments as it happens. Be clear about what that recording is: evidence of
what the Kingdom did and when, useful to the Kingdom and to anyone examining it
later. It is not a filing with any regulator, it perfects nothing, and it creates
no exemption. An obligation issued without the sequence above is not a faster
route to the same place. It is a different place.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual, prepared to organise the Kingdom's affairs and keep
its officers on defensible ground. It is not legal advice, and no attorney-client
relationship arises from it or from anything else in this system. Securities
questions turn entirely on the facts of the specific offering and on every
jurisdiction in which an offer is made; the analysis of one instrument tells you
nothing reliable about another. Engage licensed Connecticut counsel experienced in
securities matters, and an independent accountant, before offering, promising, or
accepting anything.
