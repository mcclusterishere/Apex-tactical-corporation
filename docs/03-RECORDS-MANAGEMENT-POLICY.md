# Records Management Policy

*For the Registrar General, the Clerks of the Register, and every officer who
enters anything into the registers. Read it before your first entry, and again
before each annual verification.*

Adopted as an instrument of the Kingdom under Charter Art. IV, promulgated by
the Sovereign, and recorded in the Register of Instruments under its own number.
It binds every officer who holds a commission and every principal who holds an
account.

## 1. Purpose

The registers exist so that the Kingdom can answer a question about its own
affairs with a document rather than a recollection, and so that the answer
survives examination by someone who would prefer it did not. Every rule below
serves that single end. Where a rule seems fussy, it is because the alternative
has been tested in other institutions and found to fail at the worst possible
moment.

A record confers no status and creates no right. It evidences what the Kingdom
did and when. That is a narrower claim than sovereignty and a far more durable
one, because it is true and provable, and because it does not depend on anyone
outside the Kingdom agreeing to anything.

## 2. The Registrar General

The Registrar General keeps the registers and is answerable to the Sovereign for
their integrity. The office carries five duties: to record what is brought to it
promptly and accurately; to classify each record correctly; to refuse entries
that are incomplete, undated, or unsupported; to anchor the chain and verify it;
and to report annually under section 14.

The duty to refuse matters most. A Registrar who accepts whatever is handed over
produces a register that proves nothing, because everything in it is only as
good as the least careful entry. Refusal is recorded, with its reason, and the
person bringing the entry is told what would cure it.

The Registrar cannot alter the audit trail of their own acts. This is a feature
of the system and not a matter of trust in any particular holder of the office.

## 3. Contemporaneous recording

**This is the governing principle of the whole system, and everything else in
this policy is subordinate to it.**

A record made on the day the thing happened is worth many times one assembled a
week later, and the difference is not a matter of degree. Fed. R. Evid. 803(6)
admits a record made *at or near the time* by someone with knowledge, kept in
the course of a regularly conducted activity, where making it was the regular
practice. Same-day entry is not diligence for its own sake; it is the element
the exception turns on. A file reconstructed later is a reconstruction, it will
be characterised as one, and under *Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109 (1943), a
record made because litigation is anticipated does not carry the trustworthiness
the exception assumes.

The practical difference is simpler still. On the day, the officer remembers who
was present, what was said, and what was odd about it. A week later they
remember the outline and unconsciously fill the rest from what they now believe.
Nobody involved is dishonest, and the record is still worth a fraction.

## 4. What must be recorded, and by when

| Event | Recorded by |
|---|---|
| Evidence collected; an infringement or encroachment discovered | Same day, within the hour where practicable |
| Contact with any government body — call, letter, visit, service of any paper | Same day, before the office closes |
| An instrument executed, promulgated, or amended | Same day |
| Anything that triggers a duty to preserve (see `05-EVIDENCE-AND-CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY`) | Immediately, and the hold issued before merits are assessed |
| Admission of a member; commission of an officer; ordination; vital event | Within three business days |
| Acquisition or disposition of property, real or chattel | Within three business days |
| Receipts and disbursements | Within seven days |
| Discovery of an error in an existing record | On discovery, under section 12 |

Contribution acknowledgements are separate and statutory. A donor claiming a
deduction of $250 or more must hold a contemporaneous written acknowledgement
under 26 U.S.C. § 170(f)(8) by the earlier of the date they file or the due date
of their return. The Kingdom issues acknowledgements by 31 January following the
year of the gift, which clears that deadline in every ordinary case.

## 5. Numbering

Every record receives a permanent identifier of the form `AK-XXX-000000`, where
the prefix denotes the register and the sequence is allocated from a counter
held for that register alone.

Numbers are gapless and are **never reused**, including for records that are
later voided. A gap in a register invites exactly one question — what used to be
there — and the honest answer, that nothing ever was, is indistinguishable from
the dishonest one. A voided record keeps its number and carries a void notation.
Numbers are cited in correspondence, on certificates, and in filings, and a
number that has meant two different things at two different times destroys every
document that ever cited it.

## 6. Classification

Four levels, ordered: **Public**, **Members**, **Officers**, **Sealed**.
Clearance is enforced in the database query rather than in the page, so a record
above the reader's level is indistinguishable from one that does not exist.

The test is what harm follows from circulation, and it is applied to the record
in front of you rather than to the register in general:

- **Public** where the Kingdom intends the world to be able to cite it. Publication
  creates notice, which is often more useful than confidentiality.
- **Members** where the material is the internal life of the community and no
  harm follows from any member seeing it.
- **Officers** — the default — where the material bears on the Kingdom's dealings
  and belongs to those holding commissions.
- **Sealed** where disclosure would injure a person, waive a privilege, or breach
  a cultural protocol.

Sealed is not a synonym for sensitive. It is reserved, and reserved firmly, for
the personal data of minors, ecclesiastical discipline files, counsel's work
product, financial detail, the precise locations of sacred sites, and lineage
information. Sealing anything else dilutes the category until it means nothing.
Sealing and unsealing are acts of the Sovereign or the Registrar, are recorded,
and require a stated reason like any other amendment.

## 7. Amendment

A record is amended, never overwritten in silence. Every amendment requires a
stated reason, the system refuses the change without one, and the reason is
committed to the chain along with the fields that actually moved and the full
state of the record afterwards.

Write the reason as a fact, not a euphemism. *Date of first use corrected from
the invoice at AK-EVD-000112; original entry taken from memory* is a reason.
*Updated* is not, and an unexplained change is the first thing an examiner looks
for. Amend to correct the record. Do not amend to improve the Kingdom's position
in a matter that has already arisen; that is a memorandum, it belongs in the
matter file, and it must never enter the register as though it were a routine
entry.

## 8. Voiding, and the prohibition on deletion

Nothing in the registers is ever deleted. Withdrawal is a void notation with a
stated reason, committed to the chain; the record, its history, and its
attachments remain and remain readable.

This is not squeamishness. Destroying records once a dispute is foreseeable is
spoliation. Under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e) a court that finds electronically stored
information was lost because reasonable steps were not taken may order measures
to cure the prejudice, and on a finding of intent to deprive may instruct a jury
to presume the material was unfavourable, dismiss the action, or enter default.
Connecticut recognises intentional spoliation as an independent tort, *Rizzuto v.
Davidson Ladders, Inc.*, 280 Conn. 225 (2007). The sanction is almost always
worse than the record would have been.

The system refuses to void a record under an unreleased legal hold. That refusal
is not to be worked around.

## 9. Supersession and chains of title

Where a later record replaces an earlier one — a new deed, an amended
instrument, a reissued commission — the earlier is marked superseded and linked
to its replacement. The superseded record is **not** edited to describe the new
state of affairs.

The chain of title is the sequence, not the latest entry. A register showing
only current facts can state the position; a register showing how the position
was reached can prove it, which is what is wanted when someone asks what the
Kingdom held in 2019 and on what basis.

## 10. Retention

These are minima. Nothing is destroyed at the expiry of a period; expiry only
means the Kingdom is no longer obliged to keep it.

| Category | Minimum retention |
|---|---|
| Charter, instruments, ordinances, minutes of governing acts | Permanent |
| Membership, offices, ordinations, vital records | Permanent |
| Intellectual property: registrations, first-use evidence, assignments | Permanent — the record must outlive a term measured in life plus seventy years |
| Real property and chattels: title instruments | Permanent |
| Property transaction files after disposition | Seven years after disposition |
| Financial records generally | Seven years |
| Employment tax records | Four years after the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later (26 C.F.R. § 31.6001-1) |
| Correspondence, routine | Three years |
| Correspondence concerning a transaction, dispute, or government contact | Seven years |
| Evidence and matter files | Life of the matter plus the appeal period and any period for post-judgment relief; not less than seven years |
| Data subject requests and their answers | Three years |
| The ledger, the chain, and the audit log | Permanent, never pruned |

Seven years is a prudential floor rather than a single statutory command. The
duty to keep records sufficient to establish tax liability arises under
26 U.S.C. § 6001, and the assessment period extends to six years where there has
been a substantial omission of gross income under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(e); seven
years covers that outer limit with a margin. Connecticut's ordinary limitation
periods — six years on written contract under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-576, three
years in tort under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-577 — sit comfortably inside it.

## 11. Legal holds override everything above

A legal hold suspends every retention schedule in section 10 for the records
within its scope, and it does so the moment litigation is reasonably
anticipated — not when a complaint is filed and not when counsel is retained.
Automatic deletion is suspended. Nothing in scope is voided, edited, or allowed
to expire, including material that deletes itself.

Release is in writing, with the date, the reason, and the officer releasing it.
An undocumented release is indistinguishable from an unauthorised deletion, and
will be read as one.

## 12. When a certified record turns out to be wrong

Errors happen in every register ever kept. What distinguishes a well-kept one is
what happens next: **correct forward, with a stated reason, and never silently.**

1. Stop relying on the extract. Withdraw it from any pending use the same day.
2. Establish what was wrong, when it entered the register, and who has relied on
   it since.
3. Amend under section 7, naming the error plainly in the reason.
4. Issue a corrected certified extract. It carries a new content digest and a new
   position in the ledger; the superseded certificate remains verifiable as what
   it always was.
5. Notify every person known to hold the earlier extract, in writing, identifying
   the record number, what was wrong, and the date of correction. Record that
   notification as a record.
6. Where the error touched anything filed with an agency or a court, tell Counsel
   the same day.

An error the Kingdom finds and corrects on its own initiative is an
administrative event. The same error found by an opposing party, in a document
the Kingdom certified and never revisited, is a credibility event — and
credibility, once spent, is not recovered in the matter in which it was spent.
Never send a corrected extract without saying what changed.

## 13. Backups, and the annual verification

The database and the evidence store are backed up in the same operation and
restored in the same operation; either alone proves nothing. The operational
detail sits in `07-OPERATIONS-AND-DEPLOYMENT` and the security requirements in
`06-SECURITY-AND-PRIVACY`.

The chain is verified monthly and anchored externally at least monthly, and
immediately before anything leaves the building. Annually the Registrar performs
a full verification, restores a backup to a scratch location and confirms it
works, reviews every account, reviews open holds, and certifies the result. The
certification states the head sequence and hash, the date, the anchors covering
the period, any discrepancy found, and what was done about it. A verification
that has never failed and has never been tested by an actual restore is an
assumption wearing the clothes of a control.

## 14. The Registrar's annual report

Within sixty days of the close of each year the Registrar General reports to the
Sovereign in writing, and the report is recorded as an instrument. It states
what was entered by register and by volume; every amendment and void with its
reason; every sealing and unsealing; the verification certificate under section
13; the anchors published; the holds opened, running, and released; the deadlines
met and any missed; accounts opened and closed; incidents; and the Registrar's
own assessment of where the discipline is slipping.

The last item is the point of the exercise. A report that finds nothing wrong in
a year of real activity has not been written honestly, and the Sovereign should
send it back.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual prepared to organise the Kingdom's affairs. It is not
legal advice, and no attorney-client relationship arises from it. Retention
obligations, preservation duties, and the consequences of destruction turn on the
particular facts, the forum, and the stage of any matter, and the rules differ
between federal and Connecticut practice. Engage licensed Connecticut counsel
before acting on any of it.
