# Descent, Recognition, and Citizenship

*For the Founder, and for whoever inherits this work. This memorandum answers a
question that was asked plainly and deserves a plain answer, and then spends the
rest of its length on the thing that can actually be done. It is written on the
assumption that the family history is real. The whole apparatus that accompanies
it exists to find out whether it is provable — which is a different question, and
the only one any of the doors described here will open on.*

---

## 1. The answer to the question as it was asked

**Apex Kingdom cannot be federally acknowledged as an Indian tribe. Not slowly,
not with better paperwork, not with a more sympathetic administration.** The
reason is structural and it is worth stating exactly, because a vague "that
won't work" invites years of effort spent testing it.

Federal acknowledgment through the Department of the Interior runs under
25 C.F.R. Part 83. A petitioner must satisfy **all seven** of the mandatory
criteria at § 83.11. Four of them are dispositive here:

- **§ 83.11(a) — Indian entity identification.** "The petitioner has been
  identified as an American Indian entity on a substantially continuous basis
  since 1900."
- **§ 83.11(b) — Community.** "The petitioner comprises a distinct community and
  demonstrates that it existed as a community from 1900 until the present."
- **§ 83.11(c) — Political influence or authority.** "The petitioner has
  maintained political influence or authority over its members as an autonomous
  entity from 1900 until the present."
- **§ 83.11(e) — Descent.** "The petitioner's membership consists of individuals
  who descend from a historical Indian tribe (or from historical Indian tribes
  that combined and functioned as a single autonomous political entity)."

The Charter was executed on 29 May 2025 and made effective *nunc pro tunc* to
30 October 2010. Neither date is 1900. Criteria (a), (b), and (c) each require an
unbroken documentary record running back one hundred and twenty-six years, and
nothing produced now can supply a record of a thing that did not happen. This is
not a matter of proof standards or of finding the right evidence. The years
themselves are missing.

There is also a threshold bar that does not even reach the criteria.
**25 C.F.R. § 83.4** provides that the Department will not acknowledge:

> "(a) An association, organization, corporation, or entity of any character
> formed in recent times unless the entity has only changed form by recently
> incorporating or otherwise formalizing its existing politically autonomous
> community…"

That sentence was written about bodies constituted in exactly the way Apex
Kingdom was constituted. The saving clause — an entity that "has only changed
form" — protects a community that already existed politically and has now
written itself down. It does not reach a body that came into existence when its
instrument was signed. The retroactive effective date does not help: *nunc pro
tunc* declares when a community considers itself to have begun, which is a real
and honourable thing to declare, and it is not evidence of political autonomy in
1900.

§ 83.4 goes on to bar splinter groups from recognised tribes (subsection (b)),
entities subject to terminating legislation (subsection (c)), and entities
previously denied acknowledgment (subsection (d)) — with a narrow exception
added in 2025, discussed at section 5(b) below.

That is the whole of the bad news, and it is now said. It is not a judgement
about the Founder's ancestry, the community's sincerity, or the reality of what
was done to Black and Native families by state registrars in the first half of
the twentieth century. It is a statement about what one particular federal
register can be made to do, and the answer is nothing at all for a body founded
this decade.

**Nothing in the rest of this memorandum depends on that answer.** The routes
that follow are real, several of them are open right now, and one of them has
already restored citizenship to thousands of Black Americans on precisely the
history the Founder is describing.

## 2. The distinction that decides everything

Almost every wasted year in this field comes from confusing two things that
sound alike and are not alike at all.

**A recognition claim is made by a historical community.** Its unit is a people —
a body that has existed continuously, that governed itself, that others noticed
and wrote down, that can be traced as a group across a century of hostile record-
keeping. The question asked of it is: *did you exist, continuously, as a
political community?* An organisation is not the answer to that question. An
organisation may be the vehicle a community uses to speak, but the community has
to have been there first, and the paperwork has to show it was.

**A citizenship claim is made by a family line.** Its unit is a chain of named
individuals: this person, who was the child of this person, who appears on this
roll, on this page, in this year. The question asked of it is: *are you descended
from a person this tribe already counts?* No organisation is involved on either
end. A tribe's enrollment office does not care what society the applicant
belongs to, and could not act on it if it did.

**Neither unit is a body founded in 2025.** But the second unit — the family
line — is available to the Founder today, costs comparatively little to
investigate, and is the only unit on which any of the four routes below can be
made to turn. Every register, form, and discipline in this system that touches
this subject is built around the family line for that reason.

One consequence follows immediately and should be internalised now. **The
Kingdom is not the claimant. A person is.** If the Founder's line traces to a
historical tribe, the resulting citizenship belongs to the Founder and to his
relatives individually, and it does not transfer to Apex Kingdom, to its
members generally, or to anyone the Kingdom enrols. Any document, roll, or
statement suggesting otherwise is the beginning of the failure described at
section 8.

## 3. Route A — Enrollment in an existing federally recognised tribe

### (a) Who decides, and why the answer is not negotiable

Tribes determine their own citizenship. This is not an administrative
convenience; it is the core of what tribal sovereignty means, and it was settled
by the Supreme Court in *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49 (1978),
where the Court held that the Indian Civil Rights Act does not authorise federal
suits against a tribe or its officers over a membership ordinance, and left the
Pueblo's rule standing. A tribe may set whatever criteria it chooses. It may
change them. It may close enrollment entirely.

The Bureau of Indian Affairs says the same thing in its own public guidance on
tracing ancestry: *"Tribal enrollment is determined and set by individual Tribes,
not the Bureau of Indian Affairs; therefore, uniform membership requirements
across all Tribes do not exist."*

Two practical consequences. First, **there is no appeal to a federal agency or a
federal court from a tribe's decision about its own citizens** in the ordinary
case. Second, and more usefully, **the tribe's own enrollment office is the
authority on its own rules** — not a genealogy website, not a book, not this
memorandum. Write to them. Ask what they require. Do it before assembling
anything, because they will tell you exactly which documents they accept and in
what form, and building to their specification from the start saves a year.

### (b) Base rolls

Nearly every tribe's citizenship rule runs through a **base roll** — a
historical enumeration, usually federal, that the tribe treats as the fixed
starting point. An applicant does not prove they are Indian in the abstract; they
prove **lineal descent from a specific named person on a specific roll**, and
they prove it with vital records linking each generation to the next.

Different tribes use different base rolls, taken in different decades for
different purposes. For the Five Tribes of Oklahoma it is the Dawes Rolls
(section 4). Elsewhere it may be an allotment roll, a census roll, a claims
distribution roll, or a treaty-era enumeration. Part 83 itself reflects this
practice: § 83.11(e)(1) allows a petitioner to satisfy the descent criterion by
showing that its members descend from "a tribal roll directed by Congress or
prepared by the Secretary on a descendancy basis for purposes of distributing
claims money, providing allotments, providing a tribal census, or other
purposes."

**Identify the roll before doing the genealogy.** The roll determines which
years, which geography, and which repository matter. Working without knowing the
target is how people spend four years assembling records that no tribe uses.

### (c) Lineal descent versus blood quantum

Two dominant models, and the difference between them is decisive for a family
whose Native ancestry is several generations back.

**Lineal descent.** The applicant must trace to an ancestor on the base roll.
There is no minimum fraction. The Cherokee Nation is the largest example: its
requirement is one or more direct ancestors on the Dawes Rolls, with no blood
quantum floor, so that a citizen may descend from a full-blood ancestor or from
one many generations removed.

**Blood quantum.** The applicant must possess a minimum degree — commonly one
quarter, sometimes one half, sometimes one eighth — computed from the degrees
recorded on the base roll. A family that married out for four generations can
have documented, undisputed descent and still not qualify, and no amount of
additional evidence changes that arithmetic.

A **Certificate of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB)** is a separate instrument
issued by the BIA that certifies a computed degree traced to an ancestor listed
with a degree on official tribal rolls. **A CDIB is not tribal citizenship.**
Some tribes require one, some do not, and some federal programmes ask for one
independently of enrollment. Do not let anyone conflate the two.

### (d) Honest assessment of difficulty

Where the base roll ancestor can be identified and the intervening vital records
exist, this route is administratively straightforward and comparatively cheap —
a matter of ordering certificates and filling in a form correctly. Where the
ancestor cannot be identified, or where the tribe uses blood quantum and the
family's fraction has fallen below the floor, the route is closed, and it is
closed by a rule the tribe is entitled to have.

The failure mode is not rejection. It is spending years on a tribe that was never
the right one because the family story named it and nobody checked.

## 4. Route B — Freedmen citizenship

This is the route that matters most for a Black American family with roots
touching the Five Tribes, and it deserves the most space, because it is the one
place in this entire field where a court has already held that an administrative
exclusion of Black people from tribal citizenship was unlawful and ordered it
undone.

### (a) What happened, in the order it happened

The Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole Nations held
enslaved people. After the Civil War each concluded a Reconstruction treaty with
the United States in **1866**. Each treaty abolished slavery within the nation
and addressed the status of the people it had enslaved. The terms were not
identical, and the differences between them are the reason the five nations sit
in five different places today.

Beginning in 1893 Congress created the Commission to the Five Civilized Tribes —
the **Dawes Commission** — to dissolve communal landholding and allot the land in
severalty. To allot land, the Commission had to decide who the members were, and
so it enrolled them. Applications were taken from **1898 through 1907**, with a
handful accepted in 1914. The resulting enumeration is the **Dawes Rolls**,
formally the Final Rolls of Citizens and Freedmen of the Five Civilized Tribes in
Indian Territory.

### (b) The separate schedules, and why the separation is the whole fight

The Dawes Commission did not produce one list. It produced a set of schedules,
and the National Archives records the categories under which people were
enrolled — among them:

> "Citizens by blood (BB)"; "Citizens by marriage (IM)"; "Adopted (A)";
> "Intermarried White (IW)"; "Minors by blood (M)"; "Newborns by blood (NB)";
> **"Freedmen (F)"** — described as "African Americans formerly enslaved by
> tribal members"; "Freedmen Denied/doubtful (FD)"; "Freedmen Rejected (FR)";
> "Minor Freedmen (FM)"; "Newborn Freedmen (FNB)".

Two features of that structure govern everything that has followed.

**First, no degree of Indian blood was recorded for people placed on the Freedmen
schedules.** Enrollment as a Freedman recorded a person's relation to slavery,
not their ancestry. A person with Cherokee and African parents could be — and
routinely was — enrolled as a Freedman, with the column that would have recorded
their Cherokee descent left permanently blank. The Commission's field practice
sorted people by appearance, by community report, and by former status. The
Freedmen enrollment cards carry fields for **"slave of," "father's owner," and
"mother's owner."** They do not carry a blood quantum, because the Commission was
not asking.

**Second, that blank column became the exclusion.** When the Five Tribes later
wrote citizenship rules keyed to descent from the **by-blood** schedules, the
descendants of Freedmen were excluded not by a finding about their ancestry but
by the shape of a form filled in a century earlier by a federal commission that
had already decided what they were. The mechanism is the same one Walter Plecker
used in Virginia — an administrative category applied to a person, hardening over
generations into a fact nobody can argue with, because the document says so. That
is not an analogy the Founder needs to be persuaded of. It is the same machine.

### (c) Cherokee Nation — settled, and the strongest precedent in the field

In **Cherokee Nation v. Nash, 267 F. Supp. 3d 86 (D.D.C. 2017)**, Judge Thomas F.
Hogan held on 30 August 2017 that the 1866 Treaty guarantees the descendants of
Cherokee Freedmen the same right of citizenship in the Cherokee Nation as
Cherokees by blood — that "the Cherokee Freedmen's right to citizenship in the
Cherokee Nation is directly proportional to Native Cherokees' right to
citizenship," and that a 2007 constitutional amendment restricting citizenship to
by-blood descendants could not override the treaty.

In **February 2021 the Cherokee Nation Supreme Court** ordered the words "by
blood" struck from the Cherokee Nation Constitution and its statutes, holding the
language "illegal, obsolete and repugnant to the ideal of liberty." The Cherokee
Nation accepted the ruling rather than resisting it.

**Where that leaves things today.** Cherokee Nation citizenship requires one or
more direct ancestors on the Dawes Rolls, and the Freedmen schedules count.
Press reporting at the time of the 2021 decision put the number of enrolled
citizens of Freedmen descent at roughly 8,500; that figure will have moved and is
not authority for anything. The authority is the Cherokee Nation's Tribal
Registration office, which publishes its application requirements and answers
questions about them.

This is the precedent to hold on to. It is a federal court telling a sovereign
government that a treaty made with the United States in 1866 still binds it, and
that Black descendants excluded for a century were excluded unlawfully. It is
also — and this is the part that should shape everything the Founder does — a
case that was **won on documents**. The Freedmen plaintiffs prevailed because the
Dawes Rolls exist, because the treaty text exists, and because the chain from a
named person on a named schedule to a living plaintiff could be drawn. It was not
won on the strength of the grievance, which was overwhelming and had been
overwhelming for a hundred and fifty years.

### (d) Muscogee (Creek) Nation — decided, and not yet delivered

In **Citizenship Board of the Muscogee (Creek) Nation v. Grayson**, No. SC-2023-10
(Muscogee (Creek) Nation Supreme Court, 23 July 2025), the Nation's highest court
unanimously affirmed a district court order of 27 September 2023 (No. CV-2020-34,
Okmulgee District), holding that the Citizenship Board acted contrary to law when
it denied the applications of Rhonda Grayson and Jeffrey Kennedy solely for
failure to trace to an ancestor on the Creek **by-blood** Dawes Roll, without
applying Article II of the Treaty of 1866.

What has happened since is the reason this section is written in the present
tense. Approximately a month after the ruling, the Principal Chief signed an
executive order pausing issuance of citizenship cards to Freedmen descendants,
taking the position that the court had exceeded its authority and that the
Nation's law required amendment first. Enforcement and contempt motions followed
through 2025 and into 2026. On **24 July 2026** Grayson and Kennedy filed suit in
federal court against the Principal Chief and the Citizenship Board seeking to
compel acknowledgment of their citizenship.

**Status: decided in principle, unresolved in practice, and actively in
litigation.** Anyone with a documented Creek Freedmen line should be preparing
the file now and should expect the position to change. Do not rely on this
section without checking the current state of the case.

### (e) Seminole Nation of Oklahoma — enrollment yes, equal treatment contested

Seminole Freedmen descendants may enrol, through the Nation's two Freedmen bands
— the **Caesar Bruner** and **Dosar Barkus** bands — and hold seats for those
bands on the General Council. That is genuine citizenship and it is more than the
Choctaw or Chickasaw offer.

What has been contested for decades is what the citizenship carries. Reporting and
advocacy over the past several years describe Freedmen band members being treated
as citizens for governance purposes but excluded from services and programmes
reserved to those with recorded Native ancestry. The Indian Health Service
announced in October 2021 that Seminole Freedmen are eligible for its care. Access
to housing and other tribally administered federal programmes has remained
disputed.

### (f) Choctaw and Chickasaw Nations — closed at present

Freedmen descendants are **not** eligible for citizenship in either nation today.
The historical divergence is old: the Choctaw and Chickasaw 1866 treaty made
adoption of their Freedmen conditional and held funds in trust against it; the
Choctaw Nation eventually adopted its Freedmen by tribal act in the 1880s, and
the Chickasaw Nation never did. Present-day citizenship in both nations runs to
descendants of the by-blood Dawes schedules.

*(The nineteenth-century dates in this paragraph come from secondary historical
sources and should be confirmed against the treaty text and tribal records before
being used in any filing. The statement about present eligibility is drawn from
the GAO report cited below.)*

A Choctaw-Chickasaw Freedmen advocacy effort has existed for many years and
continues to press the treaty argument. It has not yet produced the result the
Cherokee and Creek litigation produced. A documented Choctaw or Chickasaw
Freedmen line is therefore worth documenting — it is the entry ticket to any
future change, and the change may come — but it is not, today, a route to
citizenship.

### (g) The current authoritative overview

The Government Accountability Office issued **GAO-26-107118, *Tribal Programs:
Information on Freedmen Descendants of the Five Tribes***, on 15 December 2025,
publicly released 14 January 2026. It is the single best current, neutral,
citable summary of where each of the five nations stands, and it should be read
before anything else in this area. Its findings include:

- Freedmen descendants are eligible to enrol in the Cherokee Nation and the
  Seminole Nation; the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's Supreme Court has ruled that it
  must permit enrollment; the Chickasaw and Choctaw Nations do not permit it.
- GAO estimated the Freedmen descendant population at between **146,400 and
  395,400** individuals as of 2022.
- Most of the enrolled Freedmen descendants GAO interviewed reported encountering
  barriers accessing tribally administered federal services.

That last finding is worth sitting with. Enrollment is the door, not the room.

### (h) Honest assessment of difficulty

For a Cherokee Freedmen line with an identifiable Dawes ancestor and intact vital
records, this is the most tractable route in this document — comparable in
difficulty to Route A and with the law now firmly on the applicant's side. For a
Creek line it is a matter of documenting now and waiting on litigation. For a
Choctaw or Chickasaw line it is documentation without a present destination. For
any line, the hard part is the same: **finding the ancestor on the schedule.**
That is a records problem, and section 9 is about how to work it.

## 5. Route C — Federal acknowledgment of a historical community

This route is not available to Apex Kingdom (section 1). It is set out here for
two reasons: because the Founder should understand what the real thing costs, and
because if the family's descent turns out to run to a community that is itself
pursuing acknowledgment, a documented descendant becomes a person that community
has a reason to want.

### (a) The two doors

Acknowledgment can come **administratively**, through the Part 83 process
administered by Interior's Office of Federal Acknowledgment; or **legislatively**,
by an Act of Congress. (A federal court decision can also establish it, but that
is rare and turns on particular histories.) The BIA publishes the resulting list
in the Federal Register annually; the current list, at **91 Fed. Reg. 4102
(30 January 2026)**, carries 575 entities.

### (b) The administrative door, and what it has actually done

Part 83 is brutal, and the honest measure of it is what has happened to
well-documented communities with reservations older than the United States.

**Connecticut is the cautionary case, and it is on the Founder's doorstep.** The
Eastern Pequot were acknowledged in 2002 and the Schaghticoke Tribal Nation in a
Final Determination of 29 January 2004. On 12 October 2005, after the Interior
Board of Indian Appeals vacated and remanded the determinations, the Department
issued Reconsidered Final Determinations **withdrawing acknowledgment from both**.
The Schaghticoke litigated and lost: *Schaghticoke Tribal Nation v. Kempthorne*,
587 F. Supp. 2d 389 (D. Conn. 2008), aff'd, 587 F.3d 132 (2d Cir. 2009), cert.
denied (2010). These are tribes with state recognition, with reservation land held
since the seventeenth century, and with continuous documented presence. They were
acknowledged and then un-acknowledged.

**Elsewhere the pattern is duration rather than reversal.** The ACHP's own
guidance records that the Nipmuc Nation of Massachusetts began acknowledgment
efforts in 1980, two years after the regulations existed, and after a "30-plus
year attempt" remains unrecognised, holding state recognition with a tax-free
reservation and tuition benefits at state institutions but no federal status.

**One Virginia tribe did succeed administratively.** The Pamunkey Indian Tribe
received a Final Determination on 2 July 2015, published in the Federal Register
on 8 July 2015 and effective that October — the first federally recognised tribe
in Virginia.

**On re-petitioning.** The 2015 revision of Part 83 barred previously denied
petitioners from trying again. Two federal district courts held Interior's
explanation for that ban inadequate under the Administrative Procedure Act, and
Interior responded with a final rule published at **90 Fed. Reg. 3643
(15 January 2025)**, effective 14 February 2025, adding **Subpart D — Re-Petition
Authorization Process** at 25 C.F.R. §§ 83.47 *et seq*. It creates a narrow,
one-time route for an unsuccessful petitioner to seek authorisation to petition
again; a petitioner denied before 14 February 2025 must request authorisation by
14 February 2030. It does nothing for a body that has never petitioned and could
not.

### (c) The congressional door, and the worked example the Founder should study

**This is the route that succeeded on an administrative-erasure argument, and it
is the most important precedent in this memorandum after *Nash*.**

The **Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia Federal Recognition Act of
2017, Pub. L. No. 115-121** (29 January 2018), extended federal recognition to six
Virginia tribes: the Chickahominy Indian Tribe; the Chickahominy Indian Tribe —
Eastern Division; the Upper Mattaponi Tribe; the Rappahannock Tribe, Inc.; the
Monacan Indian Nation; and the Nansemond Indian Tribe. Each title of the Act
extends recognition in a single sentence, fixes the tribe's membership roll as the
most recent roll submitted to the Secretary before enactment, and bars the tribe
from gaming.

Congress acted because the administrative route was foreclosed by the very
records Part 83 requires. **Walter Plecker headed Virginia's Bureau of Vital
Statistics from 1912 to 1946** (he died in 1947). Under the Racial Integrity Act
of 1924 he reclassified Virginia Indians as "colored," rewrote birth certificates
predating 1924 that had identified individuals as Indian, and compiled and
circulated surname lists to be used by registrars in making racial
determinations. He wrote that "there are no native born Virginia Indians free
from negro intermixture." The Racial Integrity Act stood until the Supreme Court
struck it down in *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967). Part 83 asks a
petitioner to prove continuous identification as an American Indian entity from
documentary records; Plecker spent three and a half decades making those records
say something else. **The federal government's remedy for a federal criterion that
a state registrar had made impossible to satisfy was an Act of Congress.**

Now the measure of what it took. These are not small numbers and they are the
point of including them:

- The Commonwealth recognised the Chickahominy, the Chickahominy Eastern
  Division, the Rappahannock, and the Upper Mattaponi in **1983**; the Nansemond
  in **1984**; the Monacan in **1989**.
- Recognition bills were introduced in Congress and reported by the Senate
  Committee on Indian Affairs across multiple Congresses — including the 108th
  (2004), the 113th (2014), and the 114th (2015) — before enactment in the 115th.
- That is roughly **two decades of sustained legislative effort**, on top of
  decades of state-level organising, resting on communities with continuous
  documented existence for centuries.

**The most recent example is fresher and starker still.** The Lumbee Tribe of
North Carolina was recognised by North Carolina in 1885 and has sought federal
recognition since 1888. The Lumbee Act of 1956, Pub. L. No. 84-570, named them
Indian but withheld the benefits of recognition — and that very statute then
blocked their 1987 Part 83 petition, because Part 83 excludes entities subject to
congressional legislation forbidding the federal relationship (now § 83.11(g) and
§ 83.4(c)). Full recognition finally came in the **National Defense Authorization
Act for Fiscal Year 2026, Pub. L. No. 119-60** (18 December 2025), which made the
Lumbee the 575th entity on the BIA list. **One hundred and thirty-seven years.**

### (d) Honest assessment of difficulty

The administrative route takes decades, has reversed itself, and is closed to
recently formed entities as a matter of regulation. The congressional route
works, and it works for exactly the argument the Founder is making — but it works
for communities that can already document a century-plus of continuous existence
and that have spent twenty years building a bipartisan delegation, a Senate
committee record, and a body of scholarly and archival support.

The useful reading of these examples is not "so it is hopeless." It is: **the
argument about administrative erasure is a winning argument in the United States
Congress when the people making it can prove who they are.** The proof is the
work. That is what the descent apparatus is for.

## 6. Route D — State recognition

### (a) What it is, and how much it varies

State recognition is a creature of each state's own law and there is no common
standard. As of the ACHP's 2018 survey, tribal entities were recognised at state
level in at least fourteen states: Alabama, Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia,
Louisiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Jersey, New York, North
Carolina, Vermont, Virginia, and Washington. Some states run a commission with
published criteria and a petition process; some recognise by statute, tribe by
tribe, with no process at all. Confirm the current position for any state before
relying on that list.

### (b) Connecticut

**Conn. Gen. Stat. § 47-59a** recognises five tribes by name — the **Schaghticoke,
the Paucatuck Eastern Pequot, the Mashantucket Pequot, the Mohegan, and the
Golden Hill Paugussett** — as self-governing entities possessing powers and duties
over tribal members and reservations, including the power to determine tribal
membership and residency on reservation land, determine the tribal form of
government, regulate trade and commerce on the reservation, make contracts, and
determine tribal leadership in accordance with tribal practice and usage. The
Mashantucket Pequot and the Mohegan are also federally recognised. Chapter 824 of
the General Statutes also provides for the Connecticut Indian Affairs Council and
for the administration of reservation land and tribal funds.

**The point that matters, and it is the one people do not expect: Connecticut law
contains no procedure for recognising a new tribe, and none for withdrawing
recognition.** The Office of Legislative Research has said so plainly. The five
names are in the statute. Adding a sixth means persuading the General Assembly to
amend § 47-59a. **There is no petition to file, no commission to apply to, and no
administrative decision to seek.** Anyone offering to prepare a Connecticut state
recognition application is selling something that does not exist.

### (c) What state recognition does and does not confer

**It does not** create a government-to-government relationship with the United
States; make members eligible for BIA or Indian Health Service programmes; permit
land to be taken into federal trust; confer tribal sovereign immunity as a matter
of federal law; or permit gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The ACHP
is explicit that the federal government's trust responsibility and nation-to-
nation dealing run to federally recognised tribes and not to others.

**It does** carry real things, and they are worth naming because they are the
things people actually want:

- **Indian Arts and Crafts Act status.** The definition of "Indian tribe" at
  25 U.S.C. § 305e includes an Indian group formally recognised as an Indian tribe
  by a state legislature, a state commission, or another similar body vested with
  state legislative tribal recognition authority. Members of a state-recognised
  tribe may therefore lawfully market their work as Indian-produced. This is not a
  technicality; see section 8(c).
- **State-level programmes**, which vary enormously — reservation land held by
  the state, seats on a state Indian affairs body, tuition benefits at state
  institutions, dedicated arts and heritage funding.
- **Practical weight in Section 106**, where a state-recognised tribe is a more
  obvious candidate for consulting party status (section 7(a)).

### (d) One thing that must not be blurred

The Official Citation of the Connecticut General Assembly that the Kingdom holds
is genuine, is signed and sealed, and is worth being proud of. **It is not state
recognition of a tribe and must never be described as though it were.** The
General Assembly issues citations for anniversaries, retirements, and business
openings. Presented honestly it opens doors. Presented as recognition it ends the
conversation and taints everything filed alongside it. This is set out at length
in `docs/01-LEGAL-POSTURE.md` § 3(d) and is repeated here only because this is the
memorandum where the temptation will arise.

## 7. What documented descent does for you even without enrollment

A great deal of what people want recognition *for* is available on other terms,
and several of those terms are available now. None of them require enrollment,
and all of them are strengthened enormously by a documented family record.

### (a) Section 106 consulting party status

Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, now codified at
**54 U.S.C. § 306108**, requires a federal agency to take into account the effects
of its undertakings on historic properties. The process is at **36 C.F.R.
Part 800**.

Federal agencies **must** consult federally recognised Indian tribes and Native
Hawaiian organisations. Separately, an agency **may** invite others to participate
as **additional consulting parties** — **36 C.F.R. §§ 800.2(c)(5) and 800.3(f)(3)**
— where they have a **demonstrated interest** in the undertaking "due to the
nature of their legal or economic relation to the undertaking or affected
properties, or their concern with the undertaking's effects on historic
properties."

The ACHP has published a *Guide to Working with Non-Federally Recognized Tribes in
the Section 106 Process* (February 2018) saying exactly this: a federal agency
"may invite state-recognized tribes or tribes with neither federal nor state
recognition to participate in the review process as 'additional consulting
parties' based on a 'demonstrated interest.'" It notes that members of
non-recognised tribes "may be direct descendants of indigenous peoples who once
occupied an area affected by an undertaking," and that not granting consulting
party status to a party with a demonstrated interest, while lawful, "may defeat
the ultimate intent of Section 106."

**Be clear about the limits, because they are real.** The same guide states that
the decision "is a discretionary decision by the federal agency… completely
discretionary and is not done on a government-to-government basis." An agency that
declines is within its rights, and a group that is declined may still submit views
as a member of the public. There is one situation where status is not
discretionary: under **36 C.F.R. § 800.3(c)(4)**, an **applicant** for the federal
assistance, permit, licence, or approval at issue is entitled to be a consulting
party regardless of what kind of entity it is. If the Kingdom is itself the
applicant, it is a consulting party as of right.

This is available now, it costs a letter, and every grant of it is a federal
agency putting in writing that this body has a demonstrated interest in historic
properties. Record every request and every response in the Register of Government
Contacts and file the correspondence; see `docs/04-GOVERNMENT-RELATIONS-PROTOCOL.md`.

### (b) NAGPRA, and precisely where its limit falls

The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, **25 U.S.C. § 3001
et seq.**, with regulations at **43 C.F.R. Part 10** (comprehensively revised
effective January 2024 — confirm the current text before relying on any
provision), governs the repatriation of human remains and cultural items held by
federal agencies and museums receiving federal funds.

**The opening.** Under **25 U.S.C. § 3005(a)(1)**, Native American human remains
and associated funerary objects are to be expeditiously returned on the request of
a **known lineal descendant** — or of the culturally affiliated tribe or Native
Hawaiian organisation. A lineal descendant is a person, identified by
genealogical evidence. **Enrollment is not required to be a lineal descendant.**
This is a genuine, individual, federally enforceable right that turns on exactly
the kind of documentation this system is built to produce.

**The limit.** Under **§ 3005(a)(2)**, unassociated funerary objects, sacred
objects, and objects of cultural patrimony are repatriated to **Indian tribes and
Native Hawaiian organisations** — and "Indian tribe" in NAGPRA means a federally
recognised tribe. A non-recognised group has no standing as a group to claim
those categories.

So NAGPRA gives a documented lineal descendant a real claim over an identified
ancestor and gives an unrecognised organisation nothing. That asymmetry is the
whole shape of this memorandum in miniature: **the family line has rights the
organisation does not.**

### (c) Cultural and heritage institution standing

Historical society, archive, museum, and cultural centre are statuses a charitable
organisation can hold now. They carry programme funding through state humanities
and arts councils, federal cultural agencies, and private foundations; they carry
standing to be consulted; they carry the ability to serve as a repository. The
ACHP's own guidance notes the Wanapum Tribe of Washington, which has chosen not to
pursue federal recognition, is nonetheless "regularly invited to participate in
Section 106 reviews and other processes such as… NAGPRA" and maintains a heritage
centre. This track is described in `docs/01-LEGAL-POSTURE.md` § 3(d) and is the
attainable version of most of what recognition is wanted for.

### (d) Standing to participate in another community's effort

If the family line traces to a historical community that exists today — whether
federally recognised, state recognised, or petitioning — then a fully documented
descendant is a person that community may have a use for. Approach with the
documents, not with a claim: a letter that says "here is what I have found, here
are the records, here is what I have not been able to establish, and I would be
grateful for your guidance" is received very differently from one that announces a
conclusion. Communities in this position have been approached by claimants their
whole institutional lives and can distinguish the two in a paragraph.

## 8. The things that destroy a claim

Stated as protection rather than as warning. Each of these has ended real
efforts, and the first two end them permanently.

### (a) Claiming a tribe without documented descent

This is the single most damaging thing available, and it is damaging in a way
that compounds. It is not merely that the claim fails. It is that the claim,
once made, is on the record, and every subsequent approach — to an agency, to a
tribe, to a legislator, to a foundation — is now made by someone with a history of
claiming something they could not support.

The ACHP guidance identifies this from the agency's side of the table: "One
potential difficulty is when groups or individuals claim to represent Indian
tribes or present themselves as Indian (federally or non-federally recognized)
when they cannot substantiate these claims." The agency's stated remedy is to ask
recognised tribes and state Indian commissions about the claimant. That is a
reference check, and it is run by the people with the longest memories in this
field and the best reasons to be exacting.

**The discipline that answers this is simple to state and hard to keep: never
assert descent the file does not carry, and never let a description of the family
tradition drift into a description of the family record.** The apparatus records
tradition as tradition and evidence as evidence, and keeps them in different
places, precisely so this cannot happen through carelessness.

### (b) Buying "citizenship" from a self-declared entity

There is an industry selling tribal membership cards, "indigenous status,"
sovereign identification documents, and enrollment in entities that recognise
themselves. **The cards are worthless. Some of the sellers are criminals, and the
purchasers have been treated as victims and sometimes as something worse.**

Two prosecutions make the shape of it concrete. The **Kaweah Indian Nation**,
which the BIA confirms is not federally recognised, was the vehicle for a scheme
selling fraudulent tribal documents to immigrants, who paid for papers they then
attempted to use to obtain driver's licences, passports, and Social Security
cards. In the Southern District of Texas, two defendants pleaded guilty to selling
membership in the **Yamassee** tribe on representations that the documents would
prevent deportation and permit lawful work; one was sentenced to 33 months and
ordered to pay $198,795 in restitution to 144 victims.

The rule that follows: **a card issued by a body that recognises itself confers
nothing.** Presenting one to any government officer as identification is a
separate and serious problem — see `docs/11-IDENTITY-AND-CREDENTIALS.md` on what
a credential may say and what it may never look like. And by the same logic, **the
Kingdom must never issue anything of this kind itself.** An Apex Kingdom document
describing a member as Native American, as an enrolled citizen of any tribe, or as
holding any status conferred by descent, is the same object as the cards above,
whatever the Kingdom's intentions. The Roll of Citizens records membership of a
private religious society and says so on its face.

### (c) The Indian Arts and Crafts Act

If the Kingdom or any member ever sells art, craft, jewellery, textile, carving,
regalia, or any similar good, this statute applies and its criminal provisions are
not decorative.

**25 U.S.C. § 305e** and **18 U.S.C. § 1159** make it unlawful to offer or display
for sale, or sell, a good in a manner that **falsely suggests it is Indian
produced, an Indian product, or the product of a particular Indian or Indian tribe
or Indian arts and crafts organization**. The criminal penalties under § 1159 for
a first offence reach a fine of up to $250,000 or five years' imprisonment or
both for an individual, and up to $1,000,000 for a person other than an
individual; subsequent offences reach fifteen years. The civil provision at
§ 305e carries damages including not less than $1,000 for each day the offer,
display, or sale continues, and suit may be brought by the Attorney General at
the Secretary's request, by an Indian tribe, by an individual Indian, or by an
Indian arts and crafts organisation.

**Now the part that surprises people, and it is the reason this section exists.**
"Indian" for this statute means a person who **is a member of an Indian tribe**, or
who **is certified as an Indian artisan by an Indian tribe**. "Indian tribe"
includes federally recognised tribes and groups formally recognised by a state
legislature or comparable state authority. It does **not** include a person who
merely has, or can document, Native ancestry. **Documented descent is not a
defence to this statute.** A person who proves a Cherokee Freedmen line back to
the Dawes Rolls and is not yet enrolled, and holds no artisan certification, still
may not lawfully market work as Indian-produced.

The practical rule for the Kingdom: **describe the maker and the tradition
truthfully and claim no tribal origin for any good** unless the maker is an
enrolled member of a qualifying tribe or holds that tribe's artisan
certification, and the file shows which. If in doubt, say nothing about origin at
all. Nothing about a sale is worth a federal criminal exposure of this size.

### (d) The reputational cost, which has no remedy

Every route in this memorandum is discretionary at some stage. An agency official
decides whether to grant consulting party status. A tribe's enrollment office
decides how much help to give an applicant. A legislator decides whether to take a
meeting. A foundation decides whether to fund. A tribal government decides whether
to answer a letter.

Each of those people can check, and in this field they check each other. A single
overstated claim — a press release, a website line, a sentence in a grant
application saying the Kingdom is a tribe or is seeking recognition as one —
propagates into every one of those files and does not come out. There is no
appeal, no correction procedure, and no statute of limitations on a reputation.

**The Founder will get one credible run at this.** That is not a reason to be
timid; it is the reason to be exact. An institution that is known for saying only
what it can prove is an institution whose next assertion is believed.

## 9. The staged plan

Cheapest and most informative first. Each stage produces something usable even if
the next stage is never reached, and no stage requires the conclusion of a later
one.

**Stage 0 — Write down what is claimed, and label it correctly.**
Before any research, record the family tradition as tradition: what is said, by
whom, told to whom, when, and how they came to know it. Name the relative.
Attribute the statement. Date the recording. This is valuable — it is the map that
tells the research where to look — and it is **not evidence of descent**. It is
entered as traditional knowledge and never as lineage. The rule that governs here
is the one already carried on the lineage fields of the Roll of Citizens, and it
does not bend: record only what a document proves, name the document, its
repository, and its page or certificate number — and never record family belief
or oral tradition as though it were descent. On the handling of this material
generally, see `docs/12-ASSETS-AND-THE-POPULATION-REGISTER.md`, Part Two.

**Stage 1 — Interview the oldest living relatives, now.**
This is the only source in the whole programme that expires, and it expires
without notice. Record with consent, ask open questions, ask specifically for
names, places, dates, and church affiliations rather than conclusions, and ask
what documents the family holds and who has them. Do not ask leading questions
about tribes; a name suggested by the interviewer comes back as a memory.

**Stage 2 — Start with the living generation and work backward one generation at
a time.**
This is the discipline that separates a genealogy that survives scrutiny from one
that does not. **Never start at a tribe and work forward, and never jump a
generation to reach a promising name.** Each link is established before the next
is attempted: this person's death certificate names these parents; this marriage
record names this father; this census household places this child with these
adults. A chain with one unproven link is worth exactly nothing, and an examiner
will find that link before finding anything else.

**Stage 3 — Order the vital records.**
Death certificates first: they are cheap, they name parents, and they are the
bridge to the previous generation. Then marriages, then births. Connecticut vital
records are held by town registrars under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 7-36 *et seq.*; other
states have their own systems and their own fees, and the state vital records
office publishes what it requires. Record what each certificate says about race,
verbatim, including where it is blank and including where it contradicts another
document. **Especially where it contradicts another document.**

**Stage 4 — Work the federal censuses.**
Federal population schedules become public 72 years after the census (92 Stat.
915, Pub. L. No. 95-416, 5 October 1978). The **1950 census was released on
1 April 2022** and is free at the National Archives; the 1960 census follows in
2032. Trace the household backward decade by decade, 1950 to 1850.

Pay particular attention to the race column across censuses **for the same
people**. A family recorded as "Indian" in one decade and "Mulatto" or "Negro" in
the next, in the same county, in the same household, is the administrative
reclassification the Founder is describing, captured in a federal record. That
pattern is evidence in its own right, it is what the Virginia tribes put before
Congress, and it is worth documenting carefully even when it does not by itself
establish descent.

**Stage 5 — Order the DNA test early, and understand exactly what it can do.**
Order it now because it is inexpensive and takes weeks to return, so it should be
running in the background while the paper work proceeds. Then hold it to its
actual capabilities.

The BIA states the position without ambiguity: *"Blood tests and DNA tests will
not help an individual document his or her descent from a specific Federally
recognized tribe or tribal community."* No tribe accepts a commercial admixture
percentage as proof of descent, and none is obliged to. A low or absent
"Indigenous Americas" percentage does not disprove a documented ancestor five or
six generations back, because any individual inherits only a fraction of any given
ancestor's DNA and may inherit none of it. A high percentage proves nothing about
*which* people, because these tests compare against reference panels, not tribes.

**What DNA is genuinely good for, and it is a great deal:** confirming or refuting
a specific relationship between two living people, which can rescue a paper trail
that has gone ambiguous; and finding matches — cousins who descend from the same
couple and who may hold the family Bible, the photograph with names on the back,
or the certificate that was never filed with any state. **Use it to find people
who have documents. Do not use it to produce a number.**

**Stage 6 — Identify which rolls to search, then search them.**
Only once a surname, a place, and a period are established. Then:

- **The Dawes Rolls**, for a line touching the Five Tribes. Applications 1898–1907,
  a few in 1914; records in **Record Group 48** (Office of the Secretary of the
  Interior) and **Record Group 75** (Bureau of Indian Affairs); enrollment cards
  digitised in the National Archives Catalog as *Enrollment Cards, 1898–1914*
  (National Archives Identifier 251747), searchable by name, tribe, and census
  card number. Search **all** schedules, not just the by-blood ones — for a Black
  American family this is the single most important instruction in this document,
  because the family is far more likely to appear on a Freedmen schedule, and the
  Freedmen cards carry the "slave of," "father's owner," and "mother's owner"
  fields that link a person to the household that enslaved them.
- **The application jackets, not merely the roll index.** The jacket holds the
  application and its supporting documents — testimony, affidavits, correspondence
  — and it is where the actual family relationships and the Commission's reasoning
  are found. The index tells you a name exists. The jacket tells you who they
  were.
- **The earlier Cherokee Freedmen rolls**, where relevant: the **Wallace Roll**
  (compiled from investigations from about 1889 to 1896) and the **Kern-Clifton
  Roll** of 1897, both held by the National Archives, which were made precisely
  because Cherokee citizenship of formerly enslaved people was being disputed. A
  family absent from Dawes may appear on one of these, and the discrepancy is
  itself historically meaningful.
- **Whichever base roll the evidence actually points to**, for a line outside the
  Five Tribes — allotment rolls, agency censuses, claims rolls. Ask the tribe's
  enrollment office which roll governs before searching.

**Stage 7 — Record the negative findings as findings.**
Every search that returns nothing gets an entry in the ancestor's record in `LIN`:
the repository, the collection, the terms searched, the years covered, the date of
the search, and the result. The register raises a deadline where findings have
been entered with no search log behind them, because that is the shape a curated
file takes and it is visible from outside. A
file of confirmations only is a file that has been curated, and any competent
reader can tell. A file that says "searched the 1900 Choctaw Freedmen schedule for
this surname across all districts; not present" is a file that has been kept
honestly, and the confirmations in it are believed because of it. This is the same
principle that governs the Evidence Vault — see
`docs/05-EVIDENCE-AND-CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY.md` — where a disclosed gap goes to weight
and a concealed one goes to credibility.

**Stage 8 — Bring in professional help at the point it stops being tractable.**
That point is usually one of three: the paper trail dies before 1870 and the line
runs into enslavement; two documents conflict irreconcilably; or a Dawes
application jacket needs to be read against the Commission's own procedures. Engage
a credentialled genealogist — the Board for Certification of Genealogists
maintains a public roster — and ask specifically for experience with the Five
Tribes and with Freedmen research, because it is a distinct specialism with its
own record set. Pay properly. Weigh the fee against the cost of building a claim
on a link that a hostile reader dismantles in an afternoon.

**Stage 9 — Only then, approach the tribe.**
When descent is documented to a named person on a named schedule of a named roll,
write to that tribe's enrollment office. Ask what they require. Send what they
ask for and nothing else. Do not send the Charter, the citation, or anything
about Apex Kingdom: the application is a person's, the tribe's business is with
that person, and enclosing an organisation's credentials in a citizenship
application will be read as exactly what section 8(a) describes.

## 10. What the record must therefore do

The Register of Lineage and Descent (`LIN`) is built to one specification: **it
must be impossible to use it to manufacture a lineage.**

That is why it takes one record per ancestor rather than one per conclusion; why
it records the source before the finding; why it carries a search log and raises
a deadline when findings appear without one; why it holds contradicting documents
side by side rather than resolving them silently; why traditional knowledge and
documentary evidence are kept in different registers under different labels; why
it flags any tribal connection resting on family tradition or a genetic result
alone; and why a finding of "no Native ancestry established" is a permitted,
recorded, final outcome rather than a failure state.

A genealogy that records only confirmations proves nothing, because a reader
cannot tell whether the negatives were absent or removed. A genealogy that records
its own contrary evidence, its own dead ends, and its own unresolved conflicts is
believed on the positives **because** of the negatives. That is not a moral
preference. It is the mechanism by which the Freedmen plaintiffs won in *Nash* and
by which six Virginia tribes persuaded Congress: the documents were allowed to say
what they said.

The Founder is right that the erasure happened. The way to prove it is to keep a
record that an adversary cannot accuse of doing the same thing in reverse.

1. Read GAO-26-107118 in full. It is the current, neutral, authoritative map of

2. Record the family tradition as tradition, with attribution and a date, before

8. Write one letter to one federal agency requesting consulting party status in a

   piece of federal correspondence in the file.

   recognition as one, or as holding any status conferred by descent, anywhere,
   to anyone.
