# The limits of the DNA test — a doctrine on what a genome can and cannot decide

*Why this belongs in the Kingdom's teaching: a family with a contested Native
claim will sooner or later be handed a DNA result and told it settles the
question. It does not — in either direction. A test that reports "African" or
"European" haplogroups does not disprove Native descent, and a test that reports
"Native American" does not, by itself, prove tribal belonging. The science is
narrower than its marketing, its Native reference data are thin and were
gathered under a history of abuse, and no tribe enrolls by it. This document
sets out exactly where the genome's authority ends, so that a true claim is
never surrendered to a test that was never built to judge it.*

*It is the third panel of a triptych: [18] shows how the paper record was made
to lie, [21] shows how the tribal rolls were rigged, and this shows why the
newest instrument — the ancestry test — cannot be trusted to overrule either.*

*Marked throughout: **[D] DOCUMENTED**, **[C] CONTESTED / IMPRECISE**, **[F]
FRINGE — EXCLUDED**.*

## 1. A uniparental haplogroup is two lines out of hundreds

**[D]** A Y-DNA result traces exactly one path: father's father's father, straight
up the male line. An mtDNA result traces exactly one path the other way: mother's
mother's mother. Each is a single unbroken thread. Go back ten generations and a
person has on the order of **1,024 ancestors**; the two direct lines a
haplogroup test reads are **two of them.** The other ~1,022 contributed to who
the person is and left **no trace whatsoever** in either uniparental marker.

**This is doctrine because it is the exact error a family is invited to make.** A
Y result of **E-M2** (a West/Central African lineage) and an mtDNA result of
**L2a1a3** (likewise African) report the deep origin of the strict paternal and
strict maternal lines — and say **nothing at all** about the eight great-
grandparents on the other branches, or the great-great-grandmother whose people
were Native. A haplogroup cannot disprove an ancestor it never reads.

## 2. Autosomal "percentages" are estimates against a reference panel — and the Native panel is thin

**[D]** The familiar pie chart — "27% this, 4% that" — is not a measurement read
off the genome like a barcode. It is a **statistical estimate** produced by
comparing a person's autosomal DNA to a **reference panel**: a library of samples
from populations the company has decided represent "African," "European,"
"Native American," and so on. **Change the reference panel and the percentage
changes.** The number is only ever as good, and as complete, as the library it is
scored against.

**[D]** For Native American ancestry, that library is **thin and unrepresentative.**
The workhorse research panels — the Human Genome Diversity Project and the 1000
Genomes Project — represent the entire Indigenous population of the Americas with
a **small handful of populations** (in the HGDP, groups such as the Pima, Maya,
Karitiana, and Surui — South and Central American peoples). **Hundreds of North
American tribes are not in the reference data at all.** A test can only match a
person to reference samples it actually holds; ancestry from an unsampled people
has **nothing to match against** and is routinely scattered into "unassigned,"
"broadly" categories, or the nearest sampled neighbour.

**[C/D] The practical consequence, stated plainly:** a result of **"0% Native
American" is not proof of no Native ancestry.** It is consistent with real Native
descent from a people the panel does not represent, with descent too many
generations back to clear the detection threshold, or with the ancestry landing
in an adjacent bucket. Absence of evidence, from a library known to be missing
most of the relevant volumes, is not evidence of absence.

## 3. Why the Native reference data are thin — the Havasupai case

**[D]** The gaps in §2 are not an accident of sampling; they are partly a
**consequence of documented abuse**, and Native communities' resulting refusal to
participate is rational.

In the 1990s, researchers from **Arizona State University** collected roughly
**200 blood samples** from members of the **Havasupai Tribe** of the Grand Canyon,
who consented to research on **diabetes**, a scourge in the community. The samples
were subsequently used — **without the donors' consent** — for studies of
**schizophrenia, inbreeding, and ancient human population migration.** The
migration research in particular was experienced as a direct assault: it was
marshalled to support the **Bering Strait crossing** account of how people reached
the Americas, which **contradicts the Havasupai's own origin teaching** that they
originated in the canyon. Blood given to fight a disease was turned into an
argument against the people's own account of themselves.

**[D]** The Havasupai sued. In **April 2010**, the **Arizona Board of Regents
settled**, paying **$700,000** to **41 tribal members**, **returning the blood
samples**, and providing other assistance. The case became the landmark reference
for **Indigenous data sovereignty** — the principle that a people, not a
laboratory, governs the collection and use of their biological information.

**[D] The doctrinal point:** many tribes now decline genetic research entirely,
**precisely because of Havasupai.** That refusal is legitimate — and it is one
direct reason the reference panels in §2 are thin. The thinness of the "Native
American" category in a commercial test is, in part, **the footprint of a people
protecting themselves.** Reading it as "these people aren't really here" inverts
the truth.

## 4. Tribal belonging is political and relational, not molecular — TallBear

**[D]** The definitive scholarly treatment is **Kim TallBear** (enrolled
**Sisseton-Wahpeton Oyate**), *Native American DNA: Tribal Belonging and the
False Promise of Genetic Science* (University of Minnesota Press, **2013**).
TallBear's argument, faithfully stated: a genetic-ancestry test **cannot tell a
person their tribe**, and cannot make a person a citizen of a Native nation,
because **belonging to a tribe is a political and social relationship** — grounded
in kinship, community, culture, and a nation's own laws — **not a molecular
fact.** "Native American DNA," she shows, is itself partly a **construct** of the
testing industry: the markers a company labels "Native American" are chosen from
the very reference samples of §2, then sold back to consumers as an identity.

**[D]** This cuts **in both directions**, and the Kingdom teaches both edges:

- A DNA test that reports no Native markers **cannot revoke** a belonging that was
  never molecular to begin with. Enrollment turns on documented descent from a
  base roll and a nation's own decision ([21] §5; *Santa Clara Pueblo v.
  Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49 (1978)) — **not** on a percentage.
- A DNA test **also cannot confer** Native identity, or prove a sweeping claim
  about a whole people's origins, for the identical reason. The instrument that is
  too weak to disqualify a family is too weak to crown one.

## 5. The ancestry percentage and the blood quantum share an author

**[D/C]** There is a straight line from the race science of the early twentieth
century to the pie chart on a modern results page. **Blood quantum** ([21] §3)
and **Walter Plecker's** reclassifications ([18]) both rest on the same premise:
that a person can be **reduced to a fraction of a "race"** and administered on that
basis. The commercial ancestry percentage is that premise **re-issued in the
language of genomics** — friendlier, voluntary, seemingly scientific, but still
the act of **assigning a human being a set of fractional racial coordinates.**

**[D]** Population geneticists themselves caution that these continental
categories — "African," "European," "Native American" — are **statistical
groupings defined by the reference panel, not natural biological kinds.** Human
variation is overwhelmingly **clinal**: it grades continuously across geography
rather than sorting into discrete races. The percentages are a useful estimate of
**recent geographic ancestry**; they are **not** a measurement of what a person
essentially *is*.

> **[F] EXCLUDED.** The mirror-image error must be named and refused: that because
> the categories are constructed and the panels are thin, a person may **assert any
> ancestry they choose** and call the test that disagrees "fake." No. The critique
> in this document is a critique of **overreach and false precision** — it does not
> license fabrication. A genetic test is **weak evidence about specific lines and
> silent about unsampled ones**; it is not a blank cheque. The remedy for a bad
> instrument is a **better one — documents** — not the abandonment of evidence.

## 6. What a family should actually do with a DNA result

**[D] The operational rules the Kingdom files as doctrine:**

1. **Treat a haplogroup as biography of two lines**, not a verdict on the whole
   tree (§1). E-M2 and L2a1a3 describe the deep paternal and maternal threads and
   leave the contested branch untouched.
2. **Treat "0% Native" as non-probative**, given the reference panels of §2–§3.
   Do not let it retire a claim; do not cite it against yourself.
3. **Do not offer a DNA result as proof of tribal belonging** — no tribe accepts
   it and it will read as not understanding how recognition works (§4; [21] §5).
4. **Anchor the claim in documents, one ancestor at a time**: the census line, the
   deed, the death certificate, the base roll ([18]; [21] §4). That is the currency
   that has ever actually moved a recognition or a citizenship case.
5. **Keep the DNA in the file** as corroboration and as biography — it is real
   information honestly gathered — but **subordinate to the paper trail**, never
   ahead of it.

## 7. Where the line is, and why the Kingdom holds it

The genome is a real and useful witness, and the Kingdom keeps it in evidence. But
it is a **narrow** witness: it reads two lines out of a thousand, it scores the
rest against a library that is **missing most of Native America** — partly because
Native America, after **Havasupai**, chose to withhold it — and it speaks in
**percentages against a reference panel**, not in facts about belonging. **No tribe
enrolls by it.** Belonging is **political and relational**, and the test was never
built to judge it (**TallBear**).

So the discipline is symmetrical, and that is the whole point. A DNA result
**cannot defeat** a documented descent — which is the true shield a family with a
contested Native claim actually needs. And a DNA result **cannot manufacture** an
identity or prove a claim about a whole people's origins — which is the fringe this
doctrine refuses ([21] §6). The instrument is too weak for both jobs. What
carries the case is what has always carried it: **the record, read honestly, one
ancestor at a time.**
