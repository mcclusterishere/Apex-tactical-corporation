# Treasury and Financial Control

*For whoever holds the office of Treasurer, on the day they are commissioned,
and for the Sovereign and Auditor who oversee them. Read `01-LEGAL-POSTURE`
first, section 6 before approving any payment to an officer or their family, and
section 9 before paying anyone anything.*

## 1. Why double entry rather than a list of payments

A list of money in and out cannot be checked: if a figure on it is wrong, nothing
about the list says so. The error surfaces months later, when the bank holds less
than the paper claims, and nobody can then say which entry was the wrong one.

Double entry fixes that with one constraint. Every transaction is recorded twice,
as a debit and as a credit, for the same amount. Buying $100 of chairs is not
"minus 100 cash"; it is 100 debited to equipment and 100 credited to cash — the
money changed form rather than vanished. Because every entry has two sides, total
debits must always equal total credits, and when they do not, the book itself
says something was entered wrongly. That balancing constraint is what makes an
error detectable at all.

Two rules follow and cannot be switched off. An unbalanced entry is never
written; and a posted entry is never edited, a mistake being corrected by a
reversing entry so a reader sees both error and correction.

Assets and expenses are debit-normal; liabilities, net assets, and revenue are
credit-normal. Reverse that and the book balances perfectly while being entirely
wrong. Amounts are held in whole cents.

## 2. The chart of accounts

The chart is the list of buckets, numbered in blocks so the code says what kind
of thing it is. Keep it small: two hundred accounts for three staff produces
misclassification, not insight.

| Block | Type | Accounts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1000s | Asset | 1000 cash operating; 1010 cash restricted; 1100 contributions receivable; 1200 prepaid and deposits; 1500 land and buildings; 1600 furniture, equipment, vehicles; 1900 accumulated depreciation (contra-asset, credit balance) |
| 2000s | Liability | 2000 accounts payable; 2100 payroll liabilities withheld; 2200 notes and obligations payable |
| 3000s | Net assets | 3000 without donor restriction; 3100 with donor restriction |
| 4000s | Revenue | 4000 tithes and offerings; 4100 designated gifts; 4200 grants; 4300 programme and event income |
| 5000s | Expense | 5000 ministry and programme; 5100 occupancy; 5200 salaries, stipends, housing allowance; 5300 payroll taxes; 5400 charitable assistance; 5500 insurance; 5600 professional fees; 5700 intellectual property; 5900 administration |

Account 2100 is never the Kingdom's money: it is the employee's, held in trust for
the United States, and section 10 says what happens to those who spend it. Add
accounts sparingly, close rather than delete those posted to, and keep none
called miscellaneous.

## 3. Funds, and why a donor's restriction binds

A restriction attached to a gift is a term of the gift, not a preference. Where a
donor gives for the roof, the Kingdom took the money on that condition, and
spending it on salaries is a breach of trust — actionable by the donor and, in
Connecticut, by the Attorney General, who represents the public interest in
charitable gifts (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 3-125). Hence the fund travels on every
posting.

**Unrestricted** money is given for the Kingdom's purposes generally. Earmarking
it internally is a *board designation*, not a restriction, because a body may
release its own designation and a donor's it may not.

**Temporarily restricted** money is given for a stated purpose or period; the
restriction lifts when the purpose is met, and the release is itself a journal
entry. The building and benevolence funds are of this kind.

**Permanently restricted** money is endowment: principal held in perpetuity, only
income or a prudent portion spent, governed by Connecticut's Uniform Prudent
Management of Institutional Funds Act, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 45a-535 et seq. Accept
such a gift only with counsel.

Current standards show two classes on the face of the statements, with and
without donor restriction — hence accounts 3000 and 3100 — but the three-way
distinction is the substance of what the donor imposed and lives in the fund
dimension. Restricted cash sits in 1010, and the operating account never borrows
against it. Keep the donor's own words on file: a restriction that cannot be
evidenced will be called invented.

## 4. The monthly close

A fixed discipline on fixed days, finished within ten business days of month end.

Post everything first, each entry from a source document. Then reconcile every
bank and card account: agree the statement to the ledger by identifying each
difference — outstanding cheques, deposits in transit, bank charges — until no
residual remains. An unexplained difference of any size is an error or a theft;
carrying it forward makes it neither less true nor easier to find.

Then the trial balance, which confirms debits equal credits across every account.
It does not prove the book right, since two offsetting errors balance perfectly,
so the system re-checks each posted entry individually. A restricted fund gone
negative is the signature of restricted money spent elsewhere.

Then the related-party register: every payment for the month to an officer, a
founder, their household, or a business any of them controls, each matched to a
disinterested advance approval and a contemporaneous note of its basis. Ten
minutes, and the highest-value control in the calendar.

Close with a one-page statement of position and activities to the Sovereign and
Auditor, funds shown separately, and lock the period.

## 5. Segregation of duties when three people do everything

The ideal is that no one person controls a transaction end to end: whoever
authorises does not execute, whoever executes does not record, whoever records
does not reconcile. With three people that is unattainable, and a policy claiming
otherwise is one nobody follows. Do what is achievable.

- **Offerings counted by two people not of the same household**, both signing the
  count sheet before the money leaves the room; that sheet, not the deposit slip,
  is the source document.
- **The person who records receipts does not bank the deposit.**
- **No disbursement out of undeposited cash.**
- **A second signature above a low threshold**, and no signature stamp anywhere.
- **Nobody approves a payment to themselves, their household, or a business they
  control** — not once, not for a small sum, not because it was urgent.
- **Vendor and payroll master data changed only on approval by someone who does
  not pay.** A fictitious vendor is the commonest small-institution fraud, and it
  is invisible in the payments list.

Where one person must both record and reconcile, the compensating controls carry
the weight, and at this scale they are not a lesser substitute but the control
system itself. Bank statements go unopened to someone who does not write cheques
— the Auditor, or a trusted outsider — who scans for unfamiliar payees,
round-number transfers, and cheques payable to cash, and initials the first page;
that one arrangement detects most schemes a small institution faces. An outside
accountant engaged for a few hours a year reads the reconciliations, payroll
returns, and related-party list. Write down who does what.

## 6. Private inurement and excess benefit

No part of the Kingdom's net earnings may benefit a private individual, and
unlike lobbying there is no insubstantial allowance. Beneath that rule sits a
regime aimed at people rather than the institution. Under 26 U.S.C.
§ 4958, where a disqualified person — an officer, a founder, a substantial
contributor, or a member of their family, which for this purpose means their
spouse, ancestors, children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, the spouses of
those descendants, and their brothers and sisters and those siblings' spouses
(§ 4958(f)(1), (4)) — receives
an economic benefit exceeding what they gave in return, an excise tax of 25 per
cent of the excess falls on the recipient, 200 per cent if uncorrected, and 10
per cent on any manager who knowingly approved it, capped at $20,000. The tax is
personal: the Kingdom cannot pay it, and indemnifying them is itself a benefit.

The protection is procedural and cheap. Under 26 C.F.R. § 53.4958-6 a rebuttable
presumption of reasonableness arises where three conditions hold: approval by a
body with no conflict of interest, reliance on appropriate comparability data,
and adequate contemporaneous documentation. In practice that is five steps:

1. **Identify who is conflicted and have them leave the room.** A person with a
   financial interest, or whose household has one, cannot sit on the approving
   body for that item.
2. **Approve in advance**, before the arrangement is entered or the payment made.
   An approval minuted after the invoice is paid is not advance approval.
3. **Put comparability data before the body ahead of the vote** — what similar
   institutions pay for similar work, from a published survey or three comparable
   postings; for a purchase, quotes from two other vendors.
4. **Record it contemporaneously**: the terms, who was present, who recused, the
   data relied on, and the basis of the decision.
5. **File the minute** and cross-reference it from the payment record.

The three conditions together shift the burden to the government to prove the
terms unreasonable.
Miss one and it stays on the officer, personally, years later, with their memory
as the only evidence. Never compensate anyone with a share of receipts, and
collect a conflict-of-interest disclosure from every officer annually.

## 7. Political campaign intervention

Section 501(c)(3) organisations, churches included, are absolutely barred from
intervening in any political campaign for or against a candidate for public
office. There is no de minimis exception and none for a sermon: an endorsement
from the pulpit, a candidate's name in a newsletter, and a payment from any
account all fall inside it. The Kingdom may teach on issues and conduct
non-partisan voter education, inviting all candidates on equal terms. Lobbying is
permitted only as an insubstantial part of activities, and a church may not make
the § 501(h) election that would supply a numeric safe harbour.

## 8. Donor substantiation and quid pro quo disclosure

For any single contribution of $250 or more the donor may claim no deduction
unless they hold a contemporaneous written acknowledgment stating the amount and
either that no goods or services were provided or a description and good-faith
estimate of what was (26 U.S.C. § 170(f)(8)). Contemporaneous means in their
hands by the earlier of the date they file or the return's due date — so
acknowledgments go out within thirty days and, without exception, by 31 January
following the year of the gift. The test applies per contribution, not to the
annual total, and a cancelled cheque does not satisfy it.

Where a payment over $75 is part gift and part payment for goods or services — a
banquet ticket, an auction lot — the Kingdom must state in writing, at
solicitation or receipt, that only the excess over the value received is
deductible, with a good-faith estimate of that value (26 U.S.C. § 6115); failure
carries a penalty under § 6714. The Kingdom describes non-cash gifts but does not
value them: it signs Form 8283 acknowledging receipt only, and files Form 8282 if
it disposes of donated property within three years.

## 9. Payroll: minister, employee, contractor

Classify before the first payment; it cannot be fixed afterwards without amended
returns.

A **contractor** controls how and when the work is done, supplies their own
tools, and serves other clients. Collect Form W-9 before the first payment and
issue Form 1099-NEC at $600 or more in a year. Treating an employee as a
contractor to avoid withholding is the most expensive error a small religious
institution makes, and everyone having preferred it is not a defence.

A **non-minister employee** is subject to federal income tax withholding in full
— not optional, not waivable by agreement, not affected by exempt status. Two
genuine reliefs exist: services for a church are excluded from FUTA under
26 U.S.C. § 3306(c)(8), and a church opposed on religious grounds to social
security taxes may elect out of FICA for its employees under § 3121(w) by filing
Form 8274 before the first date a quarterly employment tax return is otherwise
due. The election is unavailable late, and affected employees then pay SECA on
their own wages. Take it with counsel, and tell them so.

A **minister** holds dual status: employee for income tax, self-employed for
social security. The Kingdom withholds no FICA on ministerial earnings and pays
no employer share; the minister pays SECA on them, including the housing
allowance, through quarterly estimated payments unless they ask in writing for
voluntary withholding. Whether a role is ministerial is a facts question,
answered in the Register of Ordinations while nothing is in dispute.

Connecticut binds independently: withholding registration and returns (Conn. Gen.
Stat. § 12-705 et seq.) and workers' compensation (§ 31-275 et seq.).

## 10. Trust fund taxes and personal liability

Income tax and the employee's share of FICA belong to the United States from the
moment they are withheld. Any responsible person who wilfully fails to pay them
over is personally liable for the full amount under 26 U.S.C. § 6672. Responsible
person is functional, not a title: anyone with authority over which bills get
paid — the Treasurer, the Sovereign, a spouse who signs cheques, a volunteer
bookkeeper. Wilful requires only knowing the taxes were owed and paying another
creditor first.

The assessment runs against the individual, collectible from their house, wages,
and accounts, and is excepted from discharge in bankruptcy (11 U.S.C.
§§ 507(a)(8)(C), 523(a)(1)(A)). It reaches the Founder's household. Withheld
amounts sit in 2100 and are remitted on schedule under 26 C.F.R. § 31.6302-1,
ahead of every other creditor. If the money is not there when the deposit falls
due, the payroll was unaffordable when it was run.

## 11. The housing allowance

A minister may exclude from gross income a housing allowance designated as such
by the employing body, to the extent used to provide a home and not exceeding the
fair rental value of the home furnished plus utilities (26 U.S.C. § 107; Treas.
Reg. § 1.107-1). It must be **designated in advance** — a designation adopted in
March does nothing for January — and **in writing**, by official action of the
employing body. It excludes from income tax only; the allowance remains subject
to SECA.

The routine is a standing resolution adopted each December for the year ahead,
stating a dollar amount, passed by the disinterested body under section 6, filed
as an instrument, and carried in 5200. It is excluded from box 1 of Form W-2.

## 12. What books a church keeps though it files no Form 990

Being a church excuses filings, not obligations. Section 508(c)(1)(A) excepts a
church from applying for recognition of exemption and § 6033(a)(3)(A) from filing
Form 990. Neither touches 26 U.S.C. § 6001, which requires records adequate to
establish any claimed exemption. When the question comes, ordinarily on
examination under the church inquiry procedures of § 7611, the books are the
entire answer.

Keep permanently: the Charter and amendments; minutes of every governing meeting;
the conflict-of-interest policy and disclosures; compensation approvals with
their comparability data; the general ledger and annual statements; deeds. Keep
at least seven years: source documents, bank statements and reconciliations,
payroll registers and employment tax returns, Forms W-2, 1099, W-9 and I-9, donor
records and acknowledgment copies, and grant agreements.

## 13. Two lines the Treasurer does not cross

**An obligation is a security.** A note or bond the Kingdom issues is a security
(*Reves v. Ernst & Young*, 494 U.S. 56 (1990)), as is any arrangement where
members put in money expecting a return from the Kingdom's efforts (*SEC v. W.J.
Howey Co.*, 328 U.S. 293 (1946)). Section 3(a)(4) of the Securities Act,
15 U.S.C. § 77c(a)(4), genuinely exempts securities issued by an organisation
operated exclusively for religious, charitable, or benevolent purposes and not
for pecuniary profit — church bonds are lawful — but the exemption is from
**registration only**. The antifraud provisions, § 17(a), 15 U.S.C. § 77q(a), and
Rule 10b-5, apply to every offering with no exemption on any facts; blue-sky law
applies independently, most states requiring a notice filing or their own
exemption, and Connecticut's is Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36b-2 et seq.; anyone selling
must consider broker-dealer registration; and unregistered affinity offerings to
a congregation are the commonest federal securities prosecution of religious
organisations. Nothing is offered without securities counsel.

**Internal accounts are book-keeping; moving other people's money is not.**
Handling the Kingdom's own funds — tithes, payroll, vendor payment, reimbursement
against a receipt — is not money transmission, and a closed internal account
recording what a member has contributed or is owed is book-keeping. The moment
value moves between two other parties on the Kingdom's rails, it is money
transmission: a federal felony without a licence under 18 U.S.C. § 1960, with
FinCEN registration under 31 C.F.R. § 1022.380 and Connecticut licensure under
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36a-595 et seq. This system implements no such transfers.

## 14. The financial calendar

Dates falling on a weekend or holiday move to the next business day; confirm each
against current instructions.

## What this document is not

This is an operating manual, prepared to organise the Kingdom's financial affairs
and keep its officers on defensible ground. It is not legal or tax advice, and no
attorney-client relationship arises from it. Every matter it touches turns on
specific facts and on the jurisdiction in which the question arises, and
thresholds and filing dates change. Engage licensed Connecticut counsel, and an
accountant experienced with religious organisations, before acting on any of it.
