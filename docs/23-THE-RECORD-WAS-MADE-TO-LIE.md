# The record was made to lie

*How race, prejudice, and government corruption authored the paper — and why an
adverse record is evidence against its author.*

---

*Why this is the Kingdom's central doctrine on descent: every family in this
position is eventually handed a piece of government paper and told it settles the
question. A census line that says "Negro." A birth certificate that says
"colored." A commission's stamp that says REJECTED. A laboratory result that says
no detectable Native ancestry. Each is presented as a neutral fact, and each is
treated as the end of the argument.*

*None of them is neutral. Every one of those documents was produced by a system
that had already decided what the answer would be, and in several cases by
officers who are documented to have falsified the records deliberately. This
document establishes that on the record, with citations, and then does the thing
that matters: it sets out precisely what each adverse document actually proves.*

*The finding, stated once: **an adverse racial record is evidence about the
government that made it, not about the family it was made against.** That is not
a rhetorical inversion. It is what the documented history supports, and it is the
strongest true position available.*

## 1. The spine: one drop makes you Black, a fraction unmakes you Indian

**[D]** The same federal and state governments, in the same decades, applied **two
opposite rules of racial arithmetic** to the same people.

- To **Blackness** they applied **hypodescent** — the "one drop" rule. Any
  traceable African ancestry made a person Black, entirely. The rule was
  **maximising**: it grew the population subject to slavery, and later to
  segregation, disfranchisement, and the labour regime built on both.
- To **Indianness** they applied **fractional blood quantum**. Native identity was
  a fraction that halved with each generation of intermarriage until, on paper, it
  rounded to nothing. The rule was **minimising**: it shrank the population holding
  treaty rights, allotment claims, trust land, and federal obligations.

**[D]** The asymmetry is not an accident of drafting, and it is not a modern
inference. It is a settled finding of the scholarship on how American law
constructed race — see **Ariela J. Gross, *What Blood Won't Tell: A History of Race
on Trial in America*** (Harvard, 2008); **Circe Sturm, *Blood Politics*** (California,
2002); **Arica L. Coleman, *That the Blood Stay Pure*** (Indiana, 2013).

**The indictment in one sentence:** the government used the arithmetic that
produced **more Black people** and the arithmetic that produced **fewer Indians**,
and it applied both to the same families at the same time — because one rule
produced labour and the other extinguished claims to land.

**[D]** A person of mixed African and Native descent therefore did not fall
between two categories by accident. They were **assigned** to the one that cost
the government nothing and served its interests, and the assignment was then
written down as a fact. Every later document inherited that assignment. That is
the mechanism this whole document is about.

## 2. The registrar's pen — Virginia, and the model case of falsification

**[D]** **Walter Ashby Plecker** was Virginia's first Registrar of the Bureau of
Vital Statistics, serving from **1912 to 1946**. Under the **Racial Integrity Act of
1924** (Va. Acts 1924, ch. 371), Virginia recognised **two** racial categories on
vital records — **white and colored** — with the so-called **"Pocahontas exception"**
permitting a person with **one-sixteenth or less** American Indian blood and no
other non-Caucasian ancestry to be recorded as white. The exception's practical
function is itself the tell: it sheltered elite Virginians who claimed descent
from Pocahontas while the same statute was turned against people **living as
Indians**.

**[D]** Plecker did not merely administer a category. He **instructed local
registrars, county clerks, and court officials to alter records**, directing that
Virginians who identified as Indian be recorded as **colored**, and he pursued
individual families by correspondence to enforce it. In **1943** he circulated a
letter enclosing a list of surnames of families he alleged were passing, generally
reproduced under the title *"Surnames, by Counties and Cities, of Mixed Negroid
Virginia Families Striving to Pass as 'Indian' or White."*

> that is careful where the record is thin is believed where the record is thick.*

**[D] The proof that the falsification worked is an Act of Congress.** On **29
January 2018**, Congress enacted the **Thomasina E. Jordan Indian Tribes of Virginia
Federal Recognition Act, Pub. L. 115-121**, extending federal recognition to six
Virginia tribes — Chickahominy, Eastern Chickahominy, Upper Mattaponi,
Rappahannock, Monacan, and Nansemond. **Congress had to act because the
administrative route was foreclosed.** The BIA process demands continuous
documentary evidence of Indian identity; Plecker had spent three and a half
decades making Virginia's documents say something else. **The United States
legislature has therefore already conceded, in statute, that this category of
paper record is unreliable — because a state officer corrupted it.**

## 3. North Carolina's own machinery — where this family's records are

**[D] The 1950 federal census instructed enumerators to erase exactly this
family's category.** The enumerator instructions directed that persons of **mixed
Indian and Negro blood be returned as Negro** unless Indian ancestry
**predominated** *and* the person was **accepted in the community as an Indian**.
Read what that instruction actually does: a person of genuinely mixed descent was
to be written down as Negro **by rule**, and the only escape required satisfying a
neighbour's opinion. **A census line reading "Negro" for a person of mixed descent
in 1950 is not a finding about that person's ancestry. It is the instruction being
followed.**

**[D] Halifax County is not a neutral place for this question.** The
**Haliwa-Saponi** — whose name is built from **Hali**fax and **Wa**rren, the two
counties they occupy — are a Native people in precisely this county, **recognised
by the State of North Carolina in 1965**. Their members had for generations been
recorded as "colored" on state and federal paper, and they ran their own community
school because the segregated system would not accommodate them as Indians. **The
same county, the same decades, the same classification pressure, applied to a
people whose Native identity the State of North Carolina has since formally
acknowledged.** That is a documented local precedent that the paper in this county
misclassified Native people as colored — and it is a matter of state law, not
family tradition.

**[D] North Carolina ran a eugenics programme into living memory.** The **North
Carolina Eugenics Board operated from 1933 to 1977**, authorising the sterilisation
of roughly **7,600 people**. In its later decades the programme fell
**disproportionately on Black women and on poor women**, and — unusually — it
accepted referrals from **social workers**, which extended its reach beyond
institutions into ordinary families on public assistance. In **2013 North Carolina
became the first state to enact compensation for its sterilisation victims**,
establishing a fund of **$10 million**.

**Why this belongs in a document about records:** it establishes, as a matter of
state law and state admission, that **North Carolina's official apparatus treated
Black and poor bodies as objects of state management within living memory.** The
same apparatus, in the same decades, filled in the race field on this family's
certificates. A state that has paid compensation for what it did to these families
is not owed a presumption that its paperwork about them was fair.

## 4. The commissions — what a rejection actually adjudicated

**[D] The Dawes rolls were corrupt, and Congress was told so.** The Five Tribes
were allotted under the **Curtis Act of 1898**. The leading archival authority —
**Kent Carter**, former director of the National Archives–Southwest Region, *The
Dawes Commission and the Allotment of the Five Civilized Tribes, 1893–1914*
(Ancestry, 1999) — documents bribery and fraudulent enrolment, and records that
**Congress confirmed rolls it had been told were inaccurate and the product of
widespread corruption**. Of roughly **250,000 applicants, only about 101,000 were
enrolled.**

**[D] The Freedmen separation is the sharpest documented instance of the
asymmetry in § 1.** People of African descent were placed on separate **"Freedmen"
schedules and recorded WITHOUT a blood quantum figure — even where Indian
parentage was known and even where a parent appeared on the "by blood" rolls.**
Field practice classified visibly Black applicants by **sight**. That omitted
number was not a clerical detail: every later right keyed to blood quantum was
denied to their descendants **because the column had been left blank on purpose.**

**[D] *Cherokee Nation v. Nash*, 267 F. Supp. 3d 86 (D.D.C. 2017)** is the vindication
and the precedent. The court held that the descendants of the Cherokee Freedmen —
people of African descent, listed without blood quantum — hold **full citizenship
rights in the Cherokee Nation** under the Treaty of 1866. **Black ancestry. Cherokee
citizens. Settled federal law.**

**[D] Guion Miller answered a narrow question, and a rejection is not a finding of
"no Indian ancestry."** The Eastern Cherokee applications (**NARA microfilm
publication M685**) drew **45,847 applications covering some 90,000 claimants**, of
whom **30,254 were enrolled** — Miller's report of **28 May 1909**, with a supplemental
report of **5 January 1910** adding 610 names and striking 44, for a final **30,820**.
Roughly **two-thirds were refused.**

What Miller was actually deciding was **eligibility to share in a specific fund**:
whether an applicant could prove descent from a person on the **1835 Henderson,
1848 Mullay, or 1851 Siler/Chapman** rolls, and party status under the treaties of
**1835–36 and 1846**. Applicants were refused for reasons that have **nothing to do
with whether they had Native ancestry** — an ancestor absent from those three
particular earlier lists, residence far from the Cherokee domain, or descent from a
nation other than the Eastern Cherokee.

**A Guion Miller rejection is an administrative finding that one clerk could not
tie one applicant to one prior list, under rules the government wrote, for the
purpose of dividing one sum of money.** It adjudicated **entitlement to a fund**. It
did not adjudicate **ancestry**, and it cannot be honestly cited as though it did.

## 6. The catch-22 — destroy the paper, then demand the paper as proof

**[D]** Federal acknowledgment under **25 CFR Part 83** requires, among seven
mandatory criteria (**§ 83.11**), that a petitioner has been identified as an
**American Indian entity** on a substantially continuous basis **since 1900** (a),
has existed as a distinct community (b) with political authority (c) over that
period, and that members **descend from a historical Indian tribe** (e) — "historical"
meaning, under **§ 83.1**, dating from first sustained contact with non-Indians.

**[D] Every one of those continuity criteria is a demand for exactly the
documentary record that registrars and enumerators were falsifying.** The state
wrote "colored" where the family said Indian; the census rule returned mixed
families as Negro; the commissions left the blood column blank on purpose. **Then
the federal government made the surviving paper the test.**

**[D] That this is a real defect, and not a grievance, is established by the
remedy Congress itself has used.** When the administrative route proved
impossible for the Virginia tribes *because of Plecker*, Congress recognised them
**by statute** — **Pub. L. 115-121 (2018)**. The **Lumbee Act of 1956** (Pub. L. 84-570)
is the same admission in a different form: Congress named the Lumbee as Indians
while withholding the benefits of that status, an anomaly that only makes sense as
a political accommodation of a people whose documentary record had been mangled.

**The principle that follows, stated conservatively:** where a government
deliberately corrupted the records of a people's identity, **the absence of a
continuous documentary trail is evidence of the corruption, not evidence of the
absence of the people.** Congress has acted on that principle twice.

## 7. The reframing key — what each adverse document actually proves

*This is the operative section. When any of the following is produced against a
claim of Native descent, this is the documented answer.*

**A Guion Miller REJECTION.**
It proves the applicant could not be tied to the **1835 Henderson, 1848 Mullay, or
1851 Siler/Chapman** rolls for purposes of **sharing in a fund** (§ 4). Two-thirds of
some 90,000 claimants were refused on that basis. It is a finding about
**entitlement to money under rules the government wrote**, not a finding about
ancestry — and it issued from a commission whose companion rolls Congress was
warned were the product of widespread corruption.

**A census line reading "Negro" or "colored."**
It proves **the enumerator followed the instruction** (§ 3). The 1950 instruction
required mixed Indian-and-Negro people to be returned as **Negro** unless Indian
ancestry predominated *and* the community accepted them as Indian. The line records
**the rule and the neighbour's opinion**, not the person's descent.

**A birth, marriage, or death certificate recording "colored."**
It proves **what the registrar was instructed to write** (§ 2). In Virginia, the
instruction was documented and deliberate. In North Carolina, the same two-category
regime was administered by a state that ran a eugenics board until 1977 and has
since paid compensation for it (§ 3). **A race field is a record of state policy.**

**A DNA result showing African haplogroups and little or no detectable Native
ancestry.**
It proves far less than it is claimed to (see **[22]** in full). A Y-DNA or mtDNA
haplogroup reads **two ancestral lines out of roughly a thousand** at ten
generations, and is **silent** on every other branch — including the one where a
Native ancestor would sit. Autosomal percentages are estimates against a
**reference panel** whose Native American samples are thin, partly because Native
nations withheld participation after documented research abuse. And **no federally
recognised tribe enrols by DNA**; enrolment turns on **documented descent from a
base roll** (*Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49 (1978)).

**The ABSENCE of any documentary trail.**
It proves the **least** of all, and is the most misused. Where a government
deliberately destroyed the paper record of a people's identity, **absence is the
expected result of the corruption** (§ 6) — a proposition Congress has itself acted
on, twice.

## 8. What this does not establish — and why saying so is what makes it work

**[D]** This document does **not** prove that any particular family is of Native
descent. No indictment of a record-keeper can do that, and any document claiming
otherwise would be worthless the first time it met a serious reader.

What it establishes is narrower and far more useful:

1. **The adverse paper does not carry the meaning assigned to it.** A rejection, a
   race field, a missing line, and a haplogroup do not disprove Native descent —
   individually or together — and the reasons are documented, statutory, and in two
   instances conceded by Congress.
2. **The burden should not be read against the family.** Where the state corrupted
   the record, the state's record is not entitled to the presumption of accuracy it
   would ordinarily receive.
3. **The affirmative case must still be built from documents** — a specific
   ancestor, on a specific line, one record at a time (**[18]**, **[22]** § 6). This
   document clears the ground of false disproof. **It does not substitute for
   proof.**

**[D]** That limit is deliberate and it is a weapon, not a concession. A file that
claims **everything** is discounted entirely; a file that claims **exactly what it can
prove** is believed on those points. The case set out above is strong precisely
because every part of it is checkable, and because the register says plainly where
its own evidence runs out.
