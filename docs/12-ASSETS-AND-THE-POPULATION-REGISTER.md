# Assets and the Population Register

*For the Registrar General, the Treasurer, and the Sovereign. Two subjects sit
in one memorandum because they are the two things the Kingdom must be able to
answer without hesitation — **what do we hold** and **who are we** — and because
they fail in the same way: not by anyone lying, but by nobody writing it down at
the time.*

---

## Part One — What the Kingdom holds

### 1. Title is the question; the register is not the answer

The single most important sentence in this memorandum: **recording an asset in
this register does not give the Kingdom title to it.**

Title to land comes from a deed recorded on the town land records. Title to a
vehicle comes from a certificate issued by the Department of Motor Vehicles.
Title to a bank account comes from the account agreement. Title to a copyright
comes from authorship or a written assignment signed by the transferor —
17 U.S.C. § 204(a) requires the writing, and an oral assignment of a copyright
is void, not merely hard to prove.

What this register does is different and still valuable. It is the Kingdom's own
consolidated statement of what it believes it holds, on what basis, in whose
hands, under what restriction, insured to what limit, and where the underlying
instrument can be found. That is what an auditor asks for, what an insurer asks
for, what a successor Registrar needs on the first day, and what nobody can
reconstruct after the fact.

The distinction has a practical consequence that must not be lost. For every
asset the register carries, there is an **external instrument** — a deed, a
title, an assignment, a bill of sale, a grant agreement — and the register's job
is to point at it. An asset record with no external instrument identified is not
an asset the Kingdom owns. It is an asset the Kingdom asserts it owns, which is
a different thing, and the record must say so plainly rather than leave a reader
to infer ownership from the mere fact of an entry.

### 2. How title should actually be held

The Charter speaks of the Kingdom holding property. Legally, "the Kingdom" is
not a person and cannot take title. Something must, and the choice matters.

**An unincorporated association** — the default if nothing is done — can hold
property in many states but does it badly. Members may be personally liable, the
chain of title becomes uncertain as membership changes, and banks and title
insurers dislike it.

**A religious corporation or a nonstock corporation under Connecticut law** is
the ordinary vehicle and the one to prefer. Conn. Gen. Stat. § 33-264a et seq.
governs religious corporations and societies specifically; the Revised Nonstock
Corporation Act at § 33-1000 et seq. is the general alternative. Either gives a
legal person that can take a deed, open an account, sign a lease, and be sued
without reaching the members personally.

**A charitable trust** holds property for a purpose rather than for members. It
is the right instrument for an endowment or for land meant to be inalienable —
and note that the Attorney General has standing to enforce a charitable trust in
Connecticut, which cuts both ways: it is protection against a future officer
selling the sacred site, and it is oversight the Kingdom cannot switch off.

The Register of Chartered Entities and Ministries (`ENT`) exists to record which
of these exists, what its filing numbers are, who its officers are, and which
assets it holds. **The entity holding title must be named on every asset
record.** "The Kingdom" is not an answer to that field. If the answer is not yet
known for an asset, that is a finding to act on, not a blank to leave.

### 3. Restriction is a legal fact, not a preference

An asset given for a stated purpose is bound to that purpose. A gift restricted
to the building fund cannot pay a legal bill, however urgent, and the Kingdom's
good intentions are not a defence. In Connecticut this runs through the Uniform
Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 45a-535 et
seq.), which also sets out the only lawful routes to releasing or modifying a
restriction — donor consent in a record, or a court, or for small and old funds
a notice procedure through the Attorney General.

So the restriction field is not a note. It is the term of a legal obligation, and
an asset whose restriction was never written down at the time of the gift is an
asset nobody can now honour correctly. Record the donor's words, not a summary of
them. Where the gift came with a letter, attach the letter.

The same restriction must appear on the fund in the treasury that carries the
asset's value, so that the books and the register cannot drift apart. See
`docs/08-TREASURY-AND-FINANCIAL-CONTROL.md` on fund accounting.

### 4. Custody is separate from ownership, and it is where property is lost

The Kingdom owns a thing. A person has it. Those are two facts and the second
changes far more often than the first.

The Register of Custody and Bailment (`CUS`) records the second. Every transfer
of possession — an officer taking a laptop home, a member borrowing regalia, a
contractor holding a vehicle, an archive item going out for conservation — is a
bailment, and the law of bailment turns on facts the register is designed to
capture: who handed it over, who received it, when, in what condition, on what
terms, and when it is due back.

Two rules make the difference between a register that helps and one that
comforts:

- **Condition is recorded at handover, by both sides, with photographs.** A
  dispute about damage is unwinnable without it, and the person who wants the
  record is almost never the person who has the item.
- **A return date is always set.** Indefinite custody is how institutions
  discover, years later, that nobody knows where something is and no one person
  can be said to have taken it. The register generates the deadline; someone must
  act on it.

Custody of *evidence* is a stricter regime again and lives in
`docs/05-EVIDENCE-AND-CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY.md`. Do not use the bailment register for
evidence: a break in an evidentiary chain is not merely untidy, it is fatal to
admissibility.

### 5. Intangibles: the Kingdom's most valuable holdings

For an institution like this one, the assets that matter most are not the
chattels. They are the copyrights in liturgy, teaching, music, and ceremony; the
marks under which the Kingdom is known; and the traditional and cultural material
the Kingdom stewards.

Three points that the asset register must reflect and that are lost with
depressing regularity:

**Employment is not authorship.** A work made by an employee within the scope of
employment belongs to the employer under 17 U.S.C. § 101's work-made-for-hire
definition. A work made by a volunteer, a contractor, or a visiting minister does
**not** — commissioned works qualify only within nine enumerated categories and
only with a signed writing. Most of what a religious body actually commissions
falls outside those categories. Without a written assignment, the Kingdom has at
best an implied licence and does not own the work it paid for. *Every* asset
record for a created work must identify the writing that transferred it, or state
that there is none.

**Registration is a deadline, not a formality.** Statutory damages and attorney's
fees are unavailable for infringement that begins before registration unless the
work is registered within three months of first publication (17 U.S.C. § 412),
and a U.S. work must be registered before an infringement suit can be brought at
all (§ 411(a)). The register tracks the three-month window because it is worth
real money and it closes silently.

**Cultural material is not simply owned.** Traditional knowledge and cultural
expressions may be held under obligations of secrecy or restricted transmission
that no copyright regime recognises and that the Kingdom nonetheless considers
binding. Record the community protocol alongside the legal status, and classify
the record so the protocol is enforced by the system rather than by memory. Where
sacred sites or ancestral material are involved, NAGPRA (25 U.S.C. § 3001 et
seq.) may be relevant to dealings with museums and federal agencies even where it
does not apply to the Kingdom directly.

### 6. What the register must never be turned into

The asset register records what the Kingdom holds. It must never be used to
assert an interest in property the Kingdom does not hold, and it must never
generate anything that looks like an encumbrance on someone else's property.

This is not a stylistic caution. Recording a document purporting to create a lien
or claim against another person's property without lawful basis is prosecuted in
Connecticut under the forgery provisions, which reach any written instrument that
purports to create, transfer, terminate, or otherwise affect a legal right or
interest (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 53a-137 et seq.), and it is a distinct federal
felony carrying up to ten years where the target is a federal officer or employee
on account of their official duties (18 U.S.C. § 1521). The false instrument may
also be void and removable on the owner's application, at the Kingdom's cost.
Institutions with real and sympathetic grievances have destroyed
themselves this way. Where the Kingdom believes an outsider holds property that
should be its own, the instrument is a claim brought in a court or an arbitration
the other side agreed to — not an entry in this register dressed up to look like
process.

### 7. Disposal

An asset leaves the register only by a recorded disposal naming what happened to
it, on whose authority, and for what consideration. Three traps:

- **Donated property sold within three years.** The Kingdom must file IRS Form
  8282 within 125 days of disposing of charitable deduction property that was
  reported on a Form 8283, and give the donor a copy. The register raises this
  deadline; someone must file it.
- **Restricted property.** A restricted asset generally cannot simply be sold and
  the proceeds spent freely — the restriction usually follows the proceeds. See
  § 3 above before disposing of anything restricted.
- **Disposal to an insider.** Selling an asset to an officer, a founder, or a
  relative is an excess benefit transaction unless it is at fair market value,
  independently determined, and approved by people with no stake in it —
  26 U.S.C. § 4958, and the penalties fall on the individual, not on the
  institution. Route it through dual-control approval and record the valuation.

## Part Two — Who the Kingdom is

### 8. What a population register is for

An association that cannot say who its members are cannot do anything else: it
cannot convene an assembly, count a vote, determine who may hold office, decide
who is entitled to assistance, or say whose consent it holds. The Roll of
Citizens (`CIT`) is therefore not an address book. It is the foundation every
other act of the Kingdom rests on, and its integrity problems are the ordinary
ones — not fraud, but drift.

Be exact about what the Roll is. It is the Kingdom's record of its own members. It
is not a civil register, it confers no status any government recognises, and
"citizen" here denotes membership of a private religious society and nothing
resembling nationality. The Kingdom's authority over a person on this Roll is
whatever that person consented to in writing, and over anyone not on it there is
none at all.

Three specific decisions must be made once and then held to.

**Enrolment must be an act, with a date.** Someone becomes a member on a
particular day, by a particular decision, recorded by a particular officer.
"Has been around for years" is not a status. If the Kingdom cannot say when a
person was enrolled, it cannot say they were a member on the day a vote was
taken, which is exactly when the question is asked.

**Consent must be captured at enrolment and must be separate.** The Kingdom's
internal authority over a member rests on that member's consent, and nothing
else. The Register of Consents to Jurisdiction and Arbitration exists so that
consent is a signed instrument with a date, not an inference from membership.
Where the consent includes an agreement to religious arbitration, that agreement
is enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act — genuinely so, and it is the
single most useful legal instrument the Kingdom possesses for resolving internal
disputes without a civil court. It is also the instrument most likely to be
challenged as unconscionable or as not knowingly entered, so the enrolment
record must show what the person was actually given and told.

**Departure must be as recorded as arrival.** A roll that only grows is a roll
that is wrong. Resignation, lapse, transfer, excommunication, and death each end
membership on a date, and the register carries the date and the ground. Nothing
is deleted: the record shows the person was a member from one date to another,
because that historical fact is often the one that matters.

### 9. Classification: this is the most sensitive material in the system

Everything in Part Two is personal data about identifiable living people, and
some of it is data whose disclosure could cause serious harm — a safeguarding
concern, an address that a person has fled, a health note supporting a benefit,
a sacramental record touching on family circumstances.

The classification levels are not advisory labels. They are enforced in the
database queries, and they must be set correctly at entry because nobody
re-reviews them later:

| Kind of record | Level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| That a person is on the roll, and their standing | `MEMBERS` | The community may know its own members |
| Contact details, household composition | `OFFICERS` | Needed to administer; not general knowledge |
| Addresses withheld for safety, safeguarding notes, health information, benefit need assessments | `SEALED` | Disclosure is the harm |
| Vital and sacramental records touching family circumstances | `SEALED` unless the person asks otherwise | Baptism, marriage, and burial records routinely reveal what a family has not chosen to publish |

Connecticut's breach notification statute (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 36a-701b) applies
to the Kingdom as it does to anyone else holding personal information, and the
duty runs to the people affected regardless of the Kingdom's view of its own
sovereignty. The Register of Data Subject Requests exists so that a member asking
what is held about them gets a recorded, answered request rather than an argument.

### 10. Households, benefits, and the two hazards there

**Households** (`HH`) record who lives with whom, which matters for assistance,
for pastoral care, and for assembly quorum where representation runs by
household. It is also, unavoidably, a record of family structure, and it is the
register most likely to contain something a member would be distressed to see
disclosed. Withheld addresses belong at `SEALED`, and the reason for withholding
belongs there too.

**Benefits** (`BEN`) records assistance given. Two hazards live here and both
are serious.

The first is **private benefit and inurement**. A charitable body may relieve
need; it may not distribute its resources to insiders or to a class defined by
relationship rather than by need. Assistance to an officer, a founder, or a
relative is an excess benefit transaction under 26 U.S.C. § 4958 unless it is
determined the same way it would be for anyone else, and the excise tax falls on
the individual who received it and on the managers who approved it. The way
through is unglamorous and effective: a written assistance policy adopted in
advance, an objective needs standard, a decision-maker with no relationship to
the applicant, and a record of both the standard and its application. Build the
policy before the first payment, because a policy adopted after a payment looks
exactly like what it is.

The second is **substantiation**. Assistance given in cash or near-cash is
income to the recipient unless it qualifies as a charitable payment to relieve
need; assistance given to relieve genuine need under a documented policy
generally is not. The record must show the need, not merely the payment.

### 11. Vital and sacramental records

Baptisms, marriages, ordinations, and burials are the Kingdom's own records of
its own sacraments, and it may keep them exactly as it wishes. Two boundaries:

**A religious marriage record is not a marriage licence.** In Connecticut,
marriage requires a licence issued by the registrar of the town where the
marriage is to be performed, and the officiant must be authorised under Conn.
Gen. Stat. § 46b-22. An ordained minister of the Kingdom may well qualify — that
is worth confirming in writing with the town before the first ceremony, not
after. The Kingdom's own record of a marriage is a genuine record of a genuine
religious act, and it is not evidence of a civil marriage. Say this to couples
before the ceremony, in writing. The failure mode here is a couple who believe
they are married and are not.

**A record of birth or death is not a certificate of one.** Connecticut vital
records are filed by the attending practitioner and the town registrar under
Conn. Gen. Stat. § 7-36 et seq. A Kingdom record noting that a member was born
or died is a legitimate internal record. A document styled as a birth certificate
is a forged government document and belongs to the offences described in
§ 6 above and in `docs/11-IDENTITY-AND-CREDENTIALS.md`.

Ordinations (`ORD`) deserve particular care for the opposite reason: they are
one of the Kingdom's genuinely strong legal instruments. The ministerial
exception — recognised unanimously in *Hosanna-Tabor* and extended in *Our Lady
of Guadalupe School* — keeps civil courts out of the Kingdom's decisions about
who ministers on its behalf, and the housing allowance under 26 U.S.C. § 107 is
real money. Both turn on a documented, contemporaneous ordination record showing
the ministerial function actually performed. A record made years later, in
anticipation of the argument, is worth very little.

### 12. The register is a person's record too

Every entry in Part Two is about a living person who did not necessarily choose
how they are described. Three habits:

- **Record what the person says their name is.** Not what a prior document said.
  Where a name has changed, keep both and record the date, because the historical
  entries are under the earlier name.
- **Do not record what is not needed.** The register asks for what the Kingdom
  must know to administer. An officer's curiosity is not a purpose.
- **Correction is amendment, never erasure.** A wrong entry is amended with a
  reason and both versions stay in the chain. That protects the member as much as
  the institution: a member who says "this was corrected and it is still being
  held against me" can point at the record.

## 13. The first month

1. Determine what legal entity holds title to each material asset. Where the
   answer is unknown, that is the first item of work — not a blank field.
2. Record the founding entity in `ENT` with its Connecticut filing numbers, and
   name it as titleholder on every asset record.
3. Enter every asset over a materiality threshold the Sovereign sets, with its
   external instrument identified. An asset with no instrument gets a record that
   says so.
4. Record every restriction in the donor's own words, with the letter attached.
5. Reconcile custody: for every asset, who has it right now, and is there a
   return date.
6. Confirm the roll. Enrolment date, standing, and signed consent for every
   member. Members without a recorded consent are the ones to approach first.
7. Adopt the assistance policy before the next payment out of `BEN`.
8. Set classifications deliberately on the people registers, and check that a
   `MEMBER`-role account genuinely cannot read what is marked `OFFICERS`.
