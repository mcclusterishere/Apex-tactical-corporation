# The rolls were corrupt — a doctrine on how the record itself was rigged

*Why this belongs in the Kingdom's teaching: the whole apparatus that decides who
counts as Indian in the United States runs on paper rolls compiled a century ago
by a hostile government. Those rolls contain both fraudulent entries and unjust
exclusions. That is not a grievance; it is documented history, and it is the
strongest true thing a family with a contested Native claim can stand on. It is
also the exact place where a real story has been hijacked by a fringe, so this
document draws that line in bright paint.*

*Marked throughout: **[D] DOCUMENTED**, **[C] CONTESTED / IMPRECISE**, **[F]
FRINGE — EXCLUDED**.*

---

## 1. The "Five Dollar Indians"

**[D] The fraud was real and it is in the archive.** During the **Dawes
Commission** enrollment of the Five Tribes (Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek,
Seminole) in Indian Territory, **1898–1907**, white people bribed their way onto
the tribal rolls "by blood" to get a land allotment. **Kent Carter** — former
director of the National Archives–Southwest Region and the leading archival
authority on the Dawes rolls — documents specific bribery, and records that
**Congress confirmed rolls it had been told were "wildly inaccurate and the
product of widespread corruption."** Bill Welge of the Oklahoma Historical
Society: commissioners "were not adverse to taking money under the table."
Historian Gregory Smithers: "government agents, for $5, were willing to turn a
blind eye." (Carter, *The Dawes Commission*, 1999; Alysa Landry, *Indian Country
Today*, 21 March 2017.)

**[C] What is characterisation, not proof.** There was **no fixed "$5" price
list** in the record. "$5" is a durable shorthand and a historian's phrase, not a
proven tariff. Present the *fraud* as documented; present the *exact mechanism
and magnitude* as characterisation resting partly on oral tradition.

> **[F] EXCLUDED.** "Most enrolled Cherokee today are fraudulent whites" overstates
> a real but **bounded** phenomenon and is false. Hold the line, because the
> inflated version is what gets the true one dismissed.

## 2. The larger injustice was exclusion, not fake inclusion

**[D]** The bigger, better-documented wrong was **people wrongly kept OFF.** More
than **250,000 people applied** to the Dawes Rolls; only **just over 100,000 were
enrolled** — roughly **60% rejected.** Genuine Native people, and especially
people of **mixed African and Native descent**, were shunted onto separate
**"Freedmen" rolls** — recorded **without a blood-quantum figure** — while white
applicants were written down "by blood." That missing number was not neutral: it
was later used to strip Freedmen descendants of every right keyed to blood
quantum. (National Archives, *Dawes Rolls*; Carter.)

**[D]** Correction the research forced, and it makes the case stronger: the Five
Tribes were allotted under the **Curtis Act of 1898**, not the 1887 Dawes
(General Allotment) Act, which had exempted them. Getting this right is what
separates a citable document from a dismissible one.

## 3. Blood quantum was a weapon, not a measurement

**[D/C]** "Blood quantum" — the fractional-ancestry test — was an **administrative
tool imported by the federal government**, not a traditional Native concept of
belonging. The documented scholarly reading (e.g. work summarised by the National
Congress of American Indians and numerous historians) is that fractional blood
rules functioned to **define Indians out of existence arithmetically** across
generations: each intermarriage halves the fraction until, on paper, a people
disappears without a single person having died. A tribe that traditionally
determined belonging by kinship, clan and community was handed a mathematics
designed to shrink it.

## 4. Guion Miller, and why rejection is not disproof

**[D]** The **Guion Miller Roll** (applications 1906–1909) drew about **90,000
claimants (45,847 applications)** and admitted only **30,254** — roughly
**two-thirds rejected.** It admitted only those who could prove descent from a
person on the **1835 Henderson, 1848 Mullay, or 1851 Siler/Chapman** rolls.
Thousands of people with genuine Cherokee ancestry failed **because their ancestor
was not on those particular earlier lists** — not because they were not Cherokee.

**This is doctrine for a reason:** a Guion Miller rejection in a family's past is
**not a finding of "no Native ancestry."** It is a finding that one clerk could
not tie one applicant to one prior list, under rules the government wrote.

## 5. The Freedmen won — and it is the precedent that matters most

**[D] *Cherokee Nation v. Nash*, 267 F. Supp. 3d 86 (D.D.C. 2017).** The
descendants of the **Cherokee Freedmen** — people of African descent, listed on
the Dawes Freedmen rolls **without blood quantum** — were held to be **full
citizens of the Cherokee Nation**, under the 1866 treaty. **Black ancestry.
Cherokee citizens. Settled federal law.**

**[D]** Under **Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49 (1978)**, tribes have
**sovereign authority to define their own members** — not the federal government,
and not a DNA laboratory.

**[D] The decisive practical fact:** **no federally recognised tribe uses a DNA or
genetic-ancestry test as an enrollment criterion.** Enrollment turns on
**documented descent from a person on a base roll.** A person's uniparental
haplogroups — African, European, or anything else — **do not bar a citizenship
claim.** Documented descent is the currency. DNA is not.

## 6. Where the line is, and why the Kingdom holds it

The true story here is powerful precisely because it is true: **the rolls were
corrupt, real families were excluded, blood quantum was a weapon, and Black people
of Native descent have won recognition in a federal court.** A person researching
a contested Native claim stands on solid ground citing every word of that.

**[F] What this doctrine refuses**, because it is where the real history gets
hijacked: the claim that the corruption of the rolls proves **"Black Americans are
the real / aboriginal Native Americans."** It does not. The rolls being imperfect
means the rolls are an **imperfect record of who was Native** — it does not
transfer a whole continent's indigeneity to another people. The average African
American genome carries about **0.7% Native American ancestry** (Bryc et al.,
*American Journal of Human Genetics*, 2015); individual family lines are sometimes
genuinely Native, and the way to establish that is **documents**, one ancestor at
a time. The collective claim is false, and asserting it is how a person loses the
true argument along with the false one.

The Kingdom teaches the corruption of the rolls **because it is documented**, and
teaches the limit **for the same reason.** That is the whole discipline: the case
that wins is the case that is true, and it is more than strong enough to stand on.
